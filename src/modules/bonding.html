Bonding & potential energy

Atoms bond because two atoms together have lower energy than the same two atoms apart. The energy well between them explains bond length, bond strength and what kind of bond forms.

The idea

Bring two atoms together. At first the nucleus of each starts to attract the other atom's electrons, and the energy drops. Push them closer and the two positive nuclei begin to repel each other, and the electron clouds crowd, so the energy shoots up. In between there is a sweet spot with the lowest energy. That distance is the bond length, and the depth of the well is the bond energy, the energy you must supply to pull the atoms apart.

What kind of bond forms depends on how the atoms share electrons. Two similar atoms share evenly (nonpolar covalent). A pull from one atom's higher electronegativity makes the sharing uneven (polar covalent). If a metal meets a nonmetal, the electron is taken completely and the oppositely charged ions attract (ionic). Metals share their outer electrons with all their neighbors in a "sea" (metallic).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Compare the single, double and triple carbon–carbon bonds. Which has the shortest bond and the deepest well?

    What you should see

    More shared electron pairs pull the nuclei closer and hold them harder: 154 pm and 347 kJ/mol, then 134 pm and 614 kJ/mol, then 120 pm and 839 kJ/mol. Shorter bond, stronger bond.

  2. Squeeze two hydrogen atoms to 50 pm and let go.

    What you should see

    Too close: the repulsion is huge and the potential energy is high, so the atoms spring apart past the equilibrium distance (74 pm), then back, vibrating around the bottom of the well.

  3. Compare sodium with chlorine, then carbon with hydrogen.

    What you should see

    Na and Cl: a large difference (2.23) and a metal with a nonmetal, so the electron is transferred: ionic. C and H: a difference of only 0.35, so the electrons are shared almost equally: nonpolar covalent.

Worked example

Three chlorine compounds you meet every day: chlorine gas from pool chemicals (Cl–Cl), stomach acid (H–Cl) and table salt (NaCl). Classify each bond and say which is the most polar. (EN: H 2.20, Cl 3.16, Na 0.93.)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Cl–Cl. Two identical atoms: ΔEN = 0. The electrons are shared equally: nonpolar covalent.
  2. H–Cl. Two nonmetals: they share electrons. ΔEN = 3.16 − 2.20 = 0.96, so the sharing is uneven, with the electrons pulled toward Cl: polar covalent (Cl is δ−, H is δ+).
  3. NaCl. A metal with a nonmetal. ΔEN = 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23. Sodium gives its outer electron to chlorine: ionic, Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
  4. Most polar. The larger the ΔEN, the more polar the bond, so NaCl (2.23) is the most polar and Cl–Cl (0) the least. The ionic bond is the extreme case of polar bonding.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking bond breaking releases energy. Breaking a bond always costs energy; forming one releases it.
  • Mixing up bond length (where the minimum is) with bond energy (how deep it is). Stronger bonds are usually shorter.
  • Calling H–Cl ionic because it is "polar". It is polar covalent: the electrons are shared, just unevenly.
  • Believing there is a sharp cutoff between types. Real bonds lie along a continuum; the cutoffs are conventions.
  • Forgetting that two metals bond metallically, not covalently or ionically.

Reading the curve

On a plot of potential energy against the distance between two nuclei, zero is the energy of the two separated atoms. The curve dips below zero as the atoms attract, reaches a minimum at the bond length, and rises steeply as they are pushed closer. The depth from zero down to the minimum is the bond energy (the energy needed to separate the atoms completely).

Coulomb's law is behind all of it: attraction between nuclei and the other atom's electrons, repulsion between nuclei, and repulsion between electron clouds. The minimum is where these balance. Ionic bonds follow the same logic: the higher the charges and the smaller the ions, the stronger the attraction (lattice energy).
