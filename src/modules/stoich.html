Stoichiometry & limiting reagent

A balanced equation is a recipe. Stoichiometry scales the recipe to the amounts you actually have, and tells you what runs out first.

The idea

To make a sandwich you need 2 slices of bread and 1 slice of cheese. With 10 slices of bread and 3 slices of cheese you can make only 3 sandwiches: the cheese runs out first. Bread is left over. In a reaction the same thing happens: the limiting reagent is used up first and decides how much product forms. The other reactant is in excess and some is left over.

The recipe is the balanced equation, and its coefficients are mole ratios. You can't compare grams directly (a gram of H₂ is a lot more molecules than a gram of O₂), so every stoichiometry problem passes through moles.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Make ammonia with 4 N₂ and 6 H₂. Which runs out?

    What you should see

    The recipe needs 3 H₂ for every N₂. Six H₂ can only handle 2 N₂, so H₂ is limiting: it makes 4 NH₃, and 2 N₂ are left over.

  2. Give exactly the recipe amounts: 4 N₂ and 12 H₂.

    What you should see

    Both run out at the same time: a stoichiometric mixture, with nothing left over and 8 NH₃ made.

  3. Now lose some product: set the percent yield to 75%.

    What you should see

    The theoretical yield is still 8 NH₃ (all the limiting reagent reacts on paper), but only 6 appear: the actual yield is 75% of the theoretical.

Worked example

A pilot reactor at a fertilizer plant is loaded with 10.0 g of N₂ and 3.00 g of H₂ for N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Find the limiting reagent, the theoretical yield of NH₃, and the mass of excess reactant left over.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Convert to moles. N₂: 10.0 g ÷ 28.02 g/mol = 0.357 mol. H₂: 3.00 g ÷ 2.016 g/mol = 1.49 mol.
  2. Find the limiting reagent. N₂ needs 3 mol H₂ per mol: 0.357 × 3 = 1.07 mol H₂ is needed, and 1.49 mol is available, so H₂ is in excess and N₂ is limiting.
  3. Theoretical yield. 0.357 mol N₂ × (2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂) = 0.714 mol NH₃; × 17.03 g/mol = 12.2 g NH₃.
  4. Excess left over. H₂ used = 1.071 mol × 2.016 g/mol = 2.16 g, so 3.00 − 2.16 = 0.84 g H₂ is left. (Subtracting rounded moles, 1.49 − 1.07, would give 0.85 g: keep an extra digit until the end.)

Common mistakes

  • Comparing grams of reactants to choose the limiting reagent. Convert to moles and use the coefficients.
  • Using the coefficients on grams instead of moles.
  • Basing the yield on the excess reactant. The limiting reagent controls how much can form.
  • Calling the actual yield the "theoretical yield". Theoretical is the calculated maximum; actual is what you measured.
  • Forgetting to use the balanced equation (e.g. using 1:1 when the ratio is 1:3).

Two ways to find the limiting reagent

Compare moles to coefficients: divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the smaller number belongs to the limiting reagent. For 0.357 mol N₂ (coefficient 1) and 1.49 mol H₂ (coefficient 3): 0.357 against 0.497, so N₂ is limiting. Compare yields: calculate how much product each reactant could make; the smaller amount is the real theoretical yield.

Percent yield compares what you got to what the limiting reagent could have given. It is always at most 100% in a clean experiment; a value above 100% usually means the product still contains water or another impurity.
