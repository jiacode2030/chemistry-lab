Logs & pH math

The concentration of H⁺ in water solutions spans fourteen powers of ten. A log scale squeezes that range into numbers from 0 to 14: that is all pH is.

The idea

Imagine a staircase where every step is ten times higher than the last. The step number tells you how many tens you have multiplied by, and that is what a logarithm is: log 1000 = 3 because 1000 = 10³. Going up one step on a log scale means multiplying by 10, not adding 1.

pH is the step number for hydrogen ions, with a minus sign so that more acid gives a smaller number: pH = −log[H⁺]. A solution with pH 3 has ten times as many H⁺ as one with pH 4, and a hundred times as many as pH 5.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Compare pH 3 with pH 5. How many times more H⁺ does the pH 3 solution have?

    What you should see

    [H⁺] = 10⁻³ at pH 3 and 10⁻⁵ at pH 5. The ratio is 10² = 100. Two steps on the staircase, two factors of ten.

  2. Look at the linear chart at pH 7. Can you see the bar? Now look at the log chart.

    What you should see

    On a linear scale [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M is far too small to see next to 1 M. The log chart makes every pH visible, which is the reason chemists use it.

  3. Does a pH of 3.0 have the same precision as a pH of 3? Enter 1.0e-3 for [H⁺].

    What you should see

    pH = 3.00: two significant figures in [H⁺] give two digits after the decimal point in the pH. The digits before the decimal point only locate the power of ten.

Worked example

What is the pH of 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M HCl? Then, a solution has pH 4.37. What is [H⁺]?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. HCl is a strong acid. It ionizes completely, so [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M.
  2. Take the log. log(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = log 3.2 + log 10⁻⁴ = 0.505 − 4 = −3.495.
  3. Flip the sign. pH = 3.495, which rounds to 3.49. Two significant figures in 3.2 give two decimal places.
  4. Now go backward. pH 4.37 means [H⁺] = 10−4.37. On the calculator, press 10x (or 2nd log) and enter −4.37.
  5. Answer. 10−4.37 = 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ M. Check: pH 4.37 is between 4 (10⁻⁴) and 5 (10⁻⁵), so [H⁺] should lie between them. It does.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the minus sign: pH = −log[H⁺], and [H⁺] is a number less than 1, so its log is negative.
  • Thinking pH 3 is "a little more acidic" than pH 4. It is ten times more.
  • Reporting too many decimal places. The decimals in a pH equal the significant figures in the concentration.
  • Using ln instead of log. Chemistry's pH uses log base 10.
  • Going back with the wrong key: [H⁺] = 10−pH, not −10pH or log(pH).

Why decimal places match significant figures

The number before the decimal point in a log only says which power of ten you are at (the "characteristic"). Only the digits after the decimal point carry information about the precision of the measurement (the "mantissa"). So 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M (2 significant figures) gives pH = 3.49 (2 decimal places).

Water autoionizes: [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Taking −log of both sides gives pH + pOH = 14.00. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M, so pH = 7.00: neutral.
