Equilibrium & the equilibrium constant

Many reactions run both ways. They don't finish: they settle into a balance. The equilibrium constant K says where that balance sits.

The idea

Picture a crowded dance floor with a door at each end. People walk in one door (reactants becoming products) as others walk out the other (products turning back). At first the traffic is lopsided. Over time the number of dancers inside stops changing, not because everyone stopped moving, but because the forward rate equals the reverse rate. That is dynamic equilibrium: motion continues, amounts don't change.

The equilibrium constant, K, is the ratio of products to reactants at that balance, with each concentration raised to its coefficient: for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, K = [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b). Big K (≫1) means products dominate; small K (≪1) means reactants dominate. At any moment, the same ratio computed with current concentrations is the reaction quotient Q. If Q < K the reaction runs forward; if Q > K it runs backward; if Q = K it is at equilibrium.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed reactions go all the way to products (100% yield) and then stop. Now we know many stop part-way, with reactants and products coexisting, and the ratio is fixed by K at a given temperature.

Try this

Predict first, then press Start.

  1. Start with only reactants and let it settle. Then restart with only products (same K). Do they reach the same mixture?

    What you should see

    Yes: the same ratio [B]/[A] = K. Equilibrium doesn't care which side you started from.

  2. Make K large (slide right). How much A is left at equilibrium?

    What you should see

    Almost none: with K = 10, about 9 of every 10 particles are B. A large K favors products, but at equilibrium the reaction is never 100% complete.

  3. Choose 2A ⇌ B. Why is K = [B]/[A]² and not [B]/[A]?

    What you should see

    Two A particles must come together to make each B, so [A] appears twice in the ratio (raised to its coefficient). The graph's forward-rate curve uses [A]².

Worked example

In an ammonia plant's reactor, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) reaches equilibrium with [N₂] = 0.50 M, [H₂] = 0.20 M and [NH₃] = 0.15 M. Calculate Kc. Then a valve pumps ammonia back in so that [NH₃] is suddenly 0.60 M. Which way does the reaction go?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Write the expression from the balanced equation: Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³).
  2. Plug in. Kc = (0.15)² / ((0.50)(0.20)³) = 0.0225 / (0.50 × 0.0080) = 0.0225 / 0.0040 = 5.6.
  3. Compute Q for the new mixture. Q = (0.60)² / ((0.50)(0.20)³) = 0.36 / 0.0040 = 90.
  4. Compare. Q (90) > K (5.6), so there is too much product. The reaction runs in reverse (toward reactants) until Q falls back to 5.6.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking equilibrium means equal concentrations. It means constant concentrations, with equal rates.
  • Thinking the reaction stops. Both directions keep going at the same rate.
  • Including pure solids and pure liquids in K. Their concentrations are constant and are left out (including water as a solvent).
  • Forgetting to raise concentrations to their coefficients, or putting reactants on top.
  • Believing a large K means a fast reaction. K says where the balance is, not how quickly it gets there.
  • Assuming K changes when you add reactant. Only temperature changes K.

Manipulating K

Reverse the reaction: K becomes 1/K. Multiply the coefficients by n: K becomes Kn. Add two reactions: multiply their K values. For gases you can also write Kp with partial pressures, and Kp = Kc(RT)Δn, where Δn = moles of gas in products − moles of gas in reactants.
