Moles & molar mass

Atoms are far too small to count one by one, so chemists count them by weighing. The mole is the bridge between a mass you can measure and a number of particles you can't see.

The idea

A grocer sells eggs by the dozen and rice by the kilogram, and has a rule that turns one into the other: "a dozen eggs weighs about 600 g". The chemist's dozen is the mole: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. The chemist's rule for turning a count into a mass is the molar mass: the mass of one mole, in grams. It is the number on the periodic table, with units of g/mol.

The surprise is that one mole of different substances weighs very different amounts, because their particles have different masses. The same mass of different substances holds very different numbers of particles. Try both below.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Put 1 mol of carbon against 1 mol of NaCl. Which pan is heavier, and which has more particles?

    What you should see

    The NaCl pan is much heavier (58.44 g against 12.01 g), but both pans hold exactly the same number of particles. A mole is a count, not a mass.

  2. Now balance equal masses: 24 g of carbon against 24 g of water.

    What you should see

    The beam is level because the masses are equal, but carbon has about 2 mol (24 ÷ 12.01) and water about 1.3 mol (24 ÷ 18.02). Lighter particles means more particles in the same mass.

  3. Glucose is a big molecule. How does 1 mol of glucose compare with 1 mol of water?

    What you should see

    180.16 g against 18.02 g: ten times the mass for the same number of molecules, because each glucose molecule is ten times as heavy.

Worked example

How many moles and how many molecules are in 9.00 g of water?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Find the molar mass. H₂O: 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol.
  2. Mass to moles. n = m / M = 9.00 g ÷ 18.02 g/mol = 0.499 mol.
  3. Moles to molecules. N = n × NA = 0.499 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
  4. Sense check. 9.00 g is half of 18.02 g, so half a mole and half of Avogadro's number. ✓

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a mole is a mass. It is a number of particles; the molar mass is what connects it to grams.
  • Using the molar mass of an atom for a molecule. For H₂O add up both H and the O.
  • Dividing when you should multiply (or the reverse). Check that the grams cancel.
  • Mixing "mol of molecules" with "mol of atoms". One mole of H₂O molecules contains 2 mol of H atoms.
  • Reading 6.022 × 10²³ as a mass. It has units of particles per mole.

The two conversions

Mass ↔ moles: n = m / M, where M is the molar mass in g/mol. Moles ↔ particles: N = n NA, with NA = 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. Almost every calculation later in the course runs through moles, so moles are the hub and grams and particles sit at the two ends.

The molar mass of a compound is the sum of the molar masses of its atoms, counting each as many times as it appears in the formula: for glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, it is 6(12.01) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.16 g/mol.
