Hybridization, sigma & pi bonds

Atoms can blend their own orbitals into new ones that point exactly where the bonds go. Bonds then form by overlap, end to end (sigma) or side by side (pi), and that explains why double bonds are rigid.

The idea

A carbon atom has one s and three p orbitals in its outer shell, but methane has four identical C–H bonds 109.5° apart. The p orbitals alone point 90° apart and the s orbital points nowhere, so none of them fit. Carbon hybridizes: it mixes the s and the three p orbitals into four identical sp³ hybrids that point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Like mixing paint, you get new colors with the same total amount.

The rule of thumb: count the regions of electron density (the same count as in VSEPR). Two regions: sp (180°). Three: sp² (120°). Four: sp³ (109.5°). Hybrid orbitals make σ (sigma) bonds, overlapping head-on along the line between the nuclei. Any p orbital left over can overlap sideways above and below the bond to make a π (pi) bond. A double bond is one σ plus one π; a triple bond is one σ plus two π.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Watch carbon mix one s and three p orbitals. How many hybrids come out, and where do they point?

    What you should see

    Four equivalent sp³ orbitals, 109.5° apart, pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. On the energy diagram the s level rises and the three p levels fall until all four are at the same level.

  2. Now try sp². What is left over?

    What you should see

    Three sp² hybrids in a flat triangle (120°) and one p orbital left over, standing perpendicular to the plane. That p orbital is the one that makes π bonds in ethene or formaldehyde.

  3. Twist the C=C bond in ethene to 90°.

    What you should see

    At 90° the two p orbitals no longer overlap sideways, the π bond is broken and the energy cost is large (about 270 kJ/mol). Double bonds are rigid, which is why cis and trans isomers exist. Compare ethane, where twisting costs only 12 kJ/mol.

Worked example

Formaldehyde, H₂C=O, is the preservative in biology lab specimens. Find the hybridization of carbon and count the σ and π bonds.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Lewis structure. C is bonded to two H atoms and double bonded to O: H–C(=O)–H. Carbon has no lone pairs.
  2. Count regions around C. Two single bonds and one double bond (one region): 3 regions.
  3. Hybridization. Three regions give sp² (120°, trigonal planar), leaving one unhybridized p orbital on carbon.
  4. Count bonds. Each single bond is one σ: 2 C–H σ bonds. The double bond is one σ plus one π, so in total 3 σ bonds and 1 π bond.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a double bond as two regions of electron density. It is one region: one σ plus one π.
  • Thinking hybrid orbitals are a property of molecules instead of atoms. Each atom in the molecule has its own hybridization.
  • Saying a lone pair does not count. It occupies a hybrid orbital: nitrogen in NH₃ (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) is sp³.
  • Forgetting that π bonds come only from unhybridized p orbitals. sp² carbon has one, sp carbon has two.
  • Believing π bonds are stronger than σ bonds. Head-on overlap is better: a σ bond is stronger than a π bond in the same pair of atoms.

Counting hybrids

The number of hybrid orbitals always equals the number of orbitals you mixed: 1 s + 3 p → 4 sp³; 1 s + 2 p → 3 sp²; 1 s + 1 p → 2 sp. The hybrids hold both bonding pairs and lone pairs. An atom in a molecule with 5 or 6 regions needs d orbitals (sp³d, sp³d²), an idea that goes beyond the course's usual cases.

Single bond: 1 σ. Double: 1 σ + 1 π. Triple: 1 σ + 2 π. So the number of σ bonds equals the number of bonded pairs of atoms, and the number of π bonds equals the total bond order minus the number of bonded pairs.
