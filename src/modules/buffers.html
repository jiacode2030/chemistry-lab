AP Unit 8

Buffers

Your blood stays near pH 7.4 even though you eat acidic food all day. A buffer is a chemical shock absorber.

The idea

A buffer holds both a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻) in comparable amounts. Add a strong acid and the A⁻ soaks it up (A⁻ + H₃O⁺ → HA). Add a strong base and the HA neutralizes it (HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O). Either way the dangerous H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ is converted into the weak partner, so the pH barely moves. Think of two sponges, one for acid and one for base.

The pH of a buffer comes from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). If the two are equal the pH equals the pKa. The ratio, not the absolute amounts, sets the pH; the absolute amounts set the capacity: how much strong acid or base the buffer can take before one component runs out. A buffer works best when the pH is within one unit of its pKa.

Assumption upgrade Before, adding a strong acid always meant a big pH drop. Now we know a solution containing both HA and A⁻ converts the added acid or base into the weak partner, and the pH changes only through the small change in the ratio.

Pick a buffer for a target pH

Try this

Predict first, then slide.

  1. Add a bit of strong acid to the buffer, then to plain water. Compare the pH change.

    What you should see

    The buffer pH moves by only a few hundredths, while plain water falls about 5 pH units (from 7 to roughly 2). The added H₃O⁺ is converted to HA.

  2. Make the buffer 10 times more dilute. What happens to its pH and to its resistance?

    What you should see

    The starting pH doesn't change (only the ratio matters), but the buffer's capacity shrinks tenfold, so the same amount of acid makes a much larger change. If it overloads, the pH plunges.

  3. Choose a ratio of 10:1 and then of 1:10. How far from the pKa is the pH?

    What you should see

    Exactly 1 pH unit above the pKa for 10:1 and 1 unit below for 1:10. Beyond that, the buffer is too one-sided to resist both acid and base well.

Worked example

A buffer contains 0.20 M acetic acid and 0.30 M acetate (pKa = 4.74), 1.00 L in total. (a) Find the pH. (b) Find the pH after adding 0.050 mol of HCl.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Henderson–Hasselbalch. pH = 4.74 + log(0.30 / 0.20) = 4.74 + 0.18 = 4.92.
  2. (b) Do the stoichiometry first. H₃O⁺ reacts completely with acetate: A⁻ + H₃O⁺ → HA. Moles: A⁻ = 0.30 − 0.050 = 0.25; HA = 0.20 + 0.050 = 0.25.
  3. Then equilibrium (H–H). pH = 4.74 + log(0.25 / 0.25) = 4.74.
  4. Compare. The pH fell by only 0.18, whereas 0.050 mol HCl in 1.00 L of plain water would give pH 1.30, a drop of nearly 6 units.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the stoichiometry step: when you add strong acid or base, first let it react completely, then use Henderson–Hasselbalch with the new moles.
  • Using a mixture of a strong acid and its conjugate base (HCl + NaCl) and calling it a buffer. The acid must be weak.
  • Mixing up which way the ratio goes: it is [conjugate base]/[acid]. More base means a higher pH.
  • Thinking dilution changes a buffer's pH. It changes capacity, not the ratio (to a good approximation).
  • Adding more strong acid than there is A⁻. Past that, the buffer is exhausted and the pH crashes.

Making a buffer

Three routes give the same result: mix the weak acid with a salt of its conjugate base; mix the weak base with a salt of its conjugate acid; or start with the weak acid and add less than enough strong base to neutralize it (that makes A⁻ in place). Choose a pair with a pKa within about one unit of the target pH, then set the ratio [A⁻]/[HA] = 10(pH − pKa).
