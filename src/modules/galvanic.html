Galvanic cells & cell potential

A battery is a chemical reaction pulled apart so its electrons have to travel through a wire. The voltage is how hard they are being pushed.

The idea

Put a zinc strip in copper(II) sulfate solution and the zinc dissolves while copper plates out: electrons hop directly from Zn to Cu²⁺. Now separate the two halves: zinc in one beaker, copper in another, joined by a wire. The electrons can't hop, so they flow through the wire. That flow is electricity, and you can run a light bulb on it. This is a galvanic (voltaic) cell.

The electrode where oxidation happens is the anode (it loses electrons); where reduction happens is the cathode. Mnemonic: An Ox and Red Cat. Electrons flow from anode to cathode through the wire. A salt bridge lets ions drift between the beakers to keep each solution neutral: anions toward the anode, cations toward the cathode. The cell's voltage is E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using standard reduction potentials. A positive E° means the reaction is spontaneous.

Assumption upgrade Before, the standard potential assumed 1 M solutions at 25 °C. Real cells aren't: concentrations change as the cell runs. The Nernst equation corrects for that: E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q. As reactants are used up Q rises toward K, and the voltage falls to zero: a "dead" battery is a cell at equilibrium.

Try this

Predict the anode and the voltage, then check.

  1. Zinc on the left, copper on the right. Which is the anode? What is E°cell?

    What you should see

    Zinc is the anode (oxidized: it has the lower reduction potential, −0.76 V) and copper the cathode (+0.34 V). E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V.

  2. Press Run the cell. What happens to the voltage as the cell runs down?

    What you should see

    It slowly falls: [Zn²⁺] rises and [Cu²⁺] falls, so Q rises. When Q = K the voltage reaches 0 V and the battery is dead. (K is about 10³⁷ for this cell, so it runs a long time.)

  3. Use the same metal on both sides but make one solution 100 times more dilute.

    What you should see

    A concentration cell: E° is 0 V, yet the cell still produces about 0.059 V. The dilute side is the anode (copper dissolves there, raising its concentration) and the concentrated side is the cathode.

Worked example

A cell is made from Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(0.010 M) and Cu²⁺(1.0 M) | Cu(s). Standard potentials: Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V, Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V. Find E°cell, then E, then ΔG°.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Anode and cathode. The metal with the lower reduction potential is oxidized: Zn is the anode, Cu the cathode. Reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, n = 2.
  2. Standard potential. E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode) = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.
  3. Nernst. Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.010/1.0 = 0.010. E = 1.10 − (0.0592/2) log(0.010) = 1.10 + 0.0592 = 1.16 V. (Less product than standard, so a bit more voltage.)
  4. Free energy. ΔG° = −nFE° = −(2)(96485)(1.10) = −212 000 J = −212 kJ per mole of reaction. Negative, matching the positive E°.

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the sign of E° when you multiply the half-reaction by a coefficient. Potentials are intensive: they do not change when you double the equation (but n does).
  • Subtracting in the wrong order: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, with both as reduction potentials.
  • Mixing up the direction of electron flow (anode → cathode) with conventional current, and anions/cations in the salt bridge.
  • Forgetting the sign convention for Q in Nernst: Q has products over reactants, and pure solids are left out.
  • Calling a negative E° "no reaction". It means the reverse reaction is the spontaneous one.

Connections

E°, ΔG° and K are three views of the same fact. Positive E° ⇔ negative ΔG° ⇔ K > 1. At equilibrium (a dead cell), E = 0 and Q = K. Batteries (alkaline, lithium-ion, lead-acid) are galvanic cells designed so that the cell potential stays close to constant while they discharge, and rechargeable ones are run backwards as electrolytic cells.
