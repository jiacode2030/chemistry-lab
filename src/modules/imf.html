Intermolecular forces

Molecules attract each other without bonding. How strongly decides whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature, and how hot it must get to boil.

The idea

Inside a molecule, covalent bonds hold atoms together. Between molecules, much weaker intermolecular forces (IMFs) act like faint Velcro. To boil a liquid you do not break any bonds in the molecules: you only pull the molecules away from each other. So the stronger the Velcro, the more heat is needed, and the higher the boiling point.

There are three kinds, all from Coulomb's law. London dispersion forces exist in every substance: electrons slosh around and make momentary dipoles; bigger electron clouds slosh more, so larger molecules attract more. Dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding is an especially strong dipole–dipole force when H is bonded to N, O or F and points at a lone pair on another N, O or F.

Boiling points of the hydrides

Down each group the molar mass and electron cloud grow, so dispersion forces grow and the boiling point rises. NH₃, H₂O and HF break the trend: hydrogen bonding makes them boil far above the rest of their group.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Compare argon and water at 200 K. Which clumps?

    What you should see

    At 200 K argon (boils at 87 K) is a gas of separate atoms, while water (boils at 373 K) is held together as a solid or liquid clump. Same temperature, very different attractions.

  2. Warm water slowly from 100 K up to 500 K.

    What you should see

    The cluster loosens and molecules start to escape around 373 K. The molecules themselves are unchanged: only the distances between them grow. Boiling overcomes IMFs, not covalent bonds.

  3. Compare methane with butane. Both are nonpolar: why does butane boil higher?

    What you should see

    Butane has many more electrons, so a bigger, more polarizable cloud and stronger dispersion forces. It is still a clump at 150 K when methane (boiling at 112 K) has flown apart.

Worked example

Water (H₂O, 18 g/mol) boils at 100 °C. Methane (CH₄, 16 g/mol, natural gas) is almost the same size but boils at −162 °C. Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S, 34 g/mol, the rotten-egg gas) is nearly twice as heavy as water and boils at −60 °C. Rank the three by boiling point and explain each with its strongest IMF.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. CH₄. Symmetric tetrahedron with nearly nonpolar C–H bonds, so no permanent dipole: London dispersion only. It has just 10 electrons, so those forces are weak. Lowest: −162 °C (112 K).
  2. H₂S. Bent and polar, so dipole–dipole plus dispersion. It has 18 electrons, so its dispersion forces are also stronger than methane's. S is not N, O or F, so there is no hydrogen bonding. −60 °C (213 K).
  3. H₂O. H bonded directly to O, so hydrogen bonding. Each molecule can form up to four: two through its H atoms and two through its lone pairs. 100 °C (373 K).
  4. Ranking. CH₄ < H₂S < H₂O. Mass alone would predict H₂S on top. Hydrogen bonding lifts water 160 °C above H₂S, which is why water is a liquid at room temperature.

Which IMF? 14 cases

Check for N–H, O–H or F–H, then for a net dipole, then for ions. The second picture shows the attraction.

1. Methane, CH₄ (natural gas). What attracts one particle to the next?

C–H bonds are almost nonpolar and the molecule is a symmetric tetrahedron, so there is no permanent dipole. Only dispersion forces, which is why methane boils at −162 °C.

2. Water, H₂O. What attracts one particle to the next?

An H bonded directly to O is attracted to a lone pair on the O of the next molecule: a hydrogen bond.

3. Ammonia, NH₃. What attracts one particle to the next?

N–H bonds, so hydrogen bonding. NH₃ boils at −33 °C, far above PH₃ (−88 °C), which can't hydrogen bond.

4. Hydrogen chloride, HCl. What attracts one particle to the next?

Polar: Cl pulls the bonding electrons. But Cl is not N, O or F, so the H can't hydrogen bond. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of the next: dipole–dipole.

5. Carbon dioxide, CO₂ (dry ice). What attracts one particle to the next?

Each C=O bond is polar, but the molecule is linear and the two bond dipoles cancel. Nonpolar overall: dispersion only. Dry ice sublimes at −78 °C.

6. Dimethyl ether, CH₃OCH₃. What attracts one particle to the next?

It has an O, but every H sits on a carbon. With no O–H it can't hydrogen bond with itself. It is bent at O and polar: dipole–dipole. It boils at −24 °C, while its isomer ethanol (with an O–H) boils at 78 °C.

7. Methanol, CH₃OH. What attracts one particle to the next?

The O–H hydrogen is attracted to a lone pair on the next molecule's O. Hydrogen bonding, so methanol is a liquid (boils at 65 °C) though it's about the size of ethane (−89 °C).

8. Hydrogen fluoride, HF. What attracts one particle to the next?

H bonded to F: hydrogen bonding. HF boils at 20 °C, while HCl boils at −85 °C.

9. Acetone (nail polish remover). What attracts one particle to the next?

The C=O is very polar, but no H is on the O. Dipole–dipole, not hydrogen bonding. Acetone boils at 56 °C.

10. Iodine, I₂. What attracts one particle to the next?

Nonpolar. But each I₂ has 106 electrons, a big cloud that is easy to distort, so the dispersion forces are strong enough to make iodine a solid at room temperature.

11. Na⁺ and water (salt dissolved in water). What attracts the ion to water?

An ion attracts the oppositely charged end of a polar molecule. Na⁺ pulls on the δ− oxygen of water: ion–dipole.

12. Cl⁻ and water (salt dissolved in water). What attracts the ion to water?

Cl⁻ is negative, so water turns its δ+ hydrogens toward it: ion–dipole. (Compare Na⁺, which attracts water's O end.)

13. Fluoromethane, CH₃F. What attracts one particle to the next?

F is there, but it is bonded to C, not H. The H's are all on carbon, so no hydrogen bonding. The C–F bond makes it polar: dipole–dipole.

14. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S. What attracts one particle to the next?

Bent and polar, but S is not N, O or F, so no hydrogen bonding: dipole–dipole. That's why H₂S boils at −60 °C, about 160 °C below water.

Common mistakes

  • Saying boiling breaks the bonds inside the molecule. Boiling water gives H₂O vapor, not hydrogen and oxygen.
  • Calling any molecule with H a hydrogen-bonder. The H must be bonded to N, O or F (CH₄ and HCl do not).
  • Forgetting that dispersion forces exist in every substance, and dominate in large nonpolar molecules.
  • Mixing up intermolecular forces with covalent bond strength: the covalent bonds in water are far stronger than the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.
  • Comparing boiling points without considering size: a large nonpolar molecule can beat a small polar one.

Ranking by strength

For molecules of similar size, the order is: hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion. For molecules of very different sizes, dispersion forces can win, so compare molar masses too. Ionic substances (full charges) are held by even stronger forces, and in a solution an ion is attracted to the partial charges on water (ion–dipole).

These attractions also explain surface tension, viscosity, vapor pressure and solubility. Stronger IMFs mean higher boiling point, higher viscosity, higher surface tension and lower vapor pressure at a given temperature.

Ion–dipole forces and mixtures

When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the force that pulls it apart is the ion–dipole attraction between each ion and the partial charges on water molecules: the oxygen end (δ−) surrounds a cation and the hydrogen ends (δ+) surround an anion. Ion–dipole forces are stronger than dipole–dipole or hydrogen bonds because they involve a full charge, which is why salts dissolve in polar solvents but not in nonpolar ones such as hexane. From strongest to weakest: ionic bonds > ion–dipole > hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion (for molecules of similar size).
