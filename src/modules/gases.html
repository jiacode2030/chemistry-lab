Gas laws & kinetic theory

A gas is mostly empty space with tiny particles flying around and bouncing off the walls. Pressure, volume, temperature and amount are four ways of describing the same crowd.

The idea

Pressure is the combined push of billions of particles hitting a wall. Heat the gas and the particles move faster, so they hit harder and more often. Squeeze the same particles into a smaller space and they hit the walls more often. Add more particles and there are more hits. That story is the kinetic molecular theory, and the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, is its summary.

Temperature has a precise meaning here: it is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. At the same temperature, light molecules move faster than heavy ones, but all have the same average kinetic energy. Individual speeds vary widely, following the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Halve the volume of a gas at constant temperature. What happens to the pressure?

    What you should see

    The pressure doubles (1 atm → 2 atm). The same particles in half the room hit the walls twice as often: P ∝ 1/V, Boyle's law.

  2. Heat the gas while the piston is free to move at constant pressure.

    What you should see

    Doubling the Kelvin temperature doubles the volume (24.6 L → 49.3 L). Faster particles push the piston out until the hit rate falls back to what the constant pressure needs. Temperature must be in kelvins.

  3. Squeeze 1 mol of CO₂ into a tiny volume in real-gas mode. Does it behave like an ideal gas?

    What you should see

    At 0.5 L the real pressure is about 39 atm against 49 atm for an ideal gas (Z ≈ 0.8): the attractions between molecules make the pressure lower. Squeeze much further (try 0.06 L) and the molecules' own volume takes over, so the real pressure is far higher than ideal.

  4. Compare helium, nitrogen and carbon dioxide at the same temperature.

    What you should see

    Light helium molecules are fastest and its curve is broad; heavy CO₂ molecules are slower with a narrow curve. All three have the same average kinetic energy, because it depends only on temperature.

Worked example

On a cool 10 °C morning you fill a car tire to an absolute pressure of 3.20 atm. (The gauge reads about 32 psi, because a tire gauge shows only the pressure above the 1.00 atm outside.) The tire holds 30.0 L of air. (a) How many moles of air are inside? (b) After an hour on a hot highway the air inside is at 50 °C. The tire barely stretches, so treat the volume as constant. What is the new pressure, and what will the gauge read?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Convert to kelvins. 10 °C = 283 K and 50 °C = 323 K. Gas laws only work with absolute temperature.
  2. (a) Ideal gas law. n = PV/RT = (3.20)(30.0) / (0.08206 × 283) = 96.0 / 23.22 = 4.13 mol of air. With atm and L, use R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).
  3. (b) Same n and V. P/T stays constant, so P2 = P1(T2/T1) = 3.20 × (323/283) = 3.65 atm.
  4. Gauge reading. 3.65 − 1.00 = 2.65 atm above the outside air. At 14.7 psi per atm that is about 39 psi, up from 32 psi. This is why tire makers say to check the pressure when the tires are cold.
  5. Check. T rose by a factor of 323/283 = 1.14, and so did P. ✓ Using °C would give 50/10 = 5 and a pressure of 16 atm, which is absurd. Always use kelvins.

Common mistakes

  • Using °C in gas-law problems. Always convert to kelvins: K = °C + 273.15.
  • Using the wrong value of R for the units: 0.08206 for atm and L, 8.314 for joules, 62.36 for torr and L.
  • Thinking heavier gases have more kinetic energy at the same temperature. The average KE is the same; the heavier ones are just slower.
  • Thinking all particles move at the average speed. The speeds span a wide range.
  • Expecting ideal behavior at high pressure and low temperature, where real gases deviate.

Deviations from ideal

The ideal gas law assumes particles occupy no volume and attract nothing. At high pressure the particles are crowded, their own volume matters and the pressure is higher than ideal. At low temperature the attractions pull particles together, so they hit the walls less hard and the pressure is lower than ideal. The van der Waals equation corrects for both: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. Gases with stronger IMFs (large a) and larger molecules (large b) deviate more.

Dalton's law: in a mixture, each gas contributes its own partial pressure, PA = XAPtotal, where XA is the mole fraction. The root-mean-square speed is urms = √(3RT/M) with M in kg/mol.
