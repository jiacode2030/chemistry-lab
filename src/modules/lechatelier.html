AP Unit 7

Le Châtelier's principle

Disturb a system at equilibrium and it fights back, shifting to partly undo the disturbance. Q and K tell you exactly why.

The idea

Imagine a seesaw perfectly balanced. Add a kid to one end and it tips; the other kids shuffle until the balance returns. A system at equilibrium does the same: add a substance and it shifts away from it; remove one and it shifts toward replacing it. Squeeze a gas mixture (smaller volume, higher pressure) and it shifts toward the side with fewer gas particles. Heat an endothermic reaction (treat heat as a reactant) and it shifts toward products; heat an exothermic reaction (heat is a product) and it shifts toward reactants.

Underneath it all is a simple rule: a stress changes Q (or, for temperature, changes K). If Q ≠ K the reaction shifts until Q = K again. Changing concentrations or volume changes Q but not K. Only temperature changes K. A catalyst changes neither: it only gets you to equilibrium sooner.

Assumption upgrade Before, we described equilibrium as fixed once reached. Now we see it is a balance that can be disturbed, and the shift follows from comparing Q with K, not from memorized rules alone.

Try this

Predict the shift first. The graph and the Q-vs-K bar will show you.

  1. N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ is endothermic. Heat it. Which way does it shift, and what happens to K?

    What you should see

    Toward products (more brown NO₂). K increases: temperature is the one stress that changes K itself.

  2. Squeeze the same gas mixture (reduce the volume). Which way does it go?

    What you should see

    Toward N₂O₄, the side with fewer gas particles (1 vs 2). Concentrations jump at first because the volume dropped, then the shift partly relieves the pressure. K doesn't change.

  3. Haber process: remove ammonia as it forms.

    What you should see

    Q drops below K, so the reaction shifts right to make more NH₃. Industry removes NH₃ continuously for this reason.

Worked example

Fertilizer plants make ammonia by the Haber process: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ. For each change, predict the shift and say whether K changes: (a) pump in more N₂, (b) run the reactor hotter, (c) compress the gas into a smaller volume, (d) add more iron catalyst. Then explain why plants run at about 450 °C and 200 atm.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. (a) Add N₂, a reactant. Q = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³) falls below K. The reaction shifts right, using up some of the added N₂. K is unchanged.
  2. (b) Raise T. The reaction is exothermic, so heat acts like a product. Adding heat is like adding product: shift left. Here K itself decreases.
  3. (c) Decrease the volume. Every gas concentration rises. The left side has 1 + 3 = 4 mol of gas and the right side 2 mol, so the system shifts toward fewer molecules: right. K is unchanged. (See the figure: 15 molecules become 13.)
  4. (d) Catalyst. It speeds up both directions equally: no shift, and K is unchanged. Equilibrium is just reached sooner.
  5. Why 450 °C and 200 atm? A low temperature gives a bigger K and more NH₃, but the reaction is far too slow. About 450 °C is a compromise between yield and speed. High pressure wins back yield by shifting right, and cooling the gas to drain off liquid NH₃ keeps Q below K.
  6. Summary. (a) right, (b) left with a smaller K, (c) right, (d) no shift. Only temperature changed the value of K.

Predict the shift: 14 cases

Read the stress, ask what it does to Q (or to K), then choose. The picture shows which side wins.

1. Haber process: the plant cools the gas and drains off liquid ammonia.

Taking away a product makes Q < K, so the reaction shifts right to replace it. Plants remove NH₃ nonstop for exactly this reason.

2. The ammonia reactor is run hotter.

The forward reaction is exothermic, so heat acts like a product. Adding heat is like adding product: shift left. Temperature is the only stress that changes K itself, and here K gets smaller.

3. The gas mixture is compressed into a smaller volume.

Left side: 1 + 3 = 4 mol of gas. Right side: 2 mol. Squeezing favors the side with fewer gas molecules, so it shifts right. K doesn't change.

4. Argon is pumped into the rigid reactor. The volume stays the same.

The total pressure goes up, but the amounts and volume of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ don't change, so their concentrations and partial pressures don't either. Q still equals K.

5. Fresh iron catalyst is added.

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally. It gets you to equilibrium sooner but doesn't move it, and K stays the same.

6. A sealed tube of brown NO₂ (mixed with colorless N₂O₄) goes into ice water. Forward ΔH = +57 kJ.

Endothermic forward, so heat is a reactant. Cooling takes away a reactant, so it shifts left toward colorless N₂O₄ and the tube turns paler. K decreases.

7. H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) is compressed.

2 mol of gas on each side. Squeezing raises every concentration by the same factor, and here Q is unchanged, so there is no shift.

8. Limestone decomposing in a closed kiln: more solid CaCO₃ is shoveled in.

Pure solids are left out of Q (here Q = PCO₂). Adding solid doesn't change Q, so nothing shifts.

9. A lime kiln blows the CO₂ away as it forms.

Q = PCO₂ drops below K, so more CaCO₃ breaks down: shift right. That is how quicklime (CaO) for cement is made.

10. A climber reaches high altitude, where each breath holds less O₂. (Hb is hemoglobin.)

Less of a reactant: shift left, so less oxygen is carried on hemoglobin. Over weeks the body makes more hemoglobin, which pushes it back to the right.

11. Solid KSCN is dissolved in the solution. FeSCN²⁺ is blood red.

Adding SCN⁻, a reactant, makes Q < K: shift right. The solution turns a deeper red. (K⁺ is a spectator.)

12. A few drops of AgNO₃ are added to the red solution. Ag⁺ and SCN⁻ form a white AgSCN precipitate.

The precipitate removes SCN⁻, a reactant. Q > K, so it shifts left and the red color fades.

13. A pink cobalt chloride solution is put in a hot water bath. Co(H₂O)₆²⁺ is pink; CoCl₄²⁻ is blue.

The forward reaction is endothermic, so heat is a reactant. Adding heat shifts right: the solution turns blue, and K increases. Cool it and it turns pink again.

14. You open a bottle of soda. CO₂ gas above the liquid escapes.

Losing CO₂ gas lowers its pressure above the soda, so Q > K. The system shifts left: dissolved CO₂ comes out as bubbles.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a concentration change or a volume change changes K. Only temperature does.
  • Thinking a catalyst shifts the equilibrium. It speeds up both directions equally and only helps reach equilibrium sooner.
  • Applying the "fewer moles" rule when the number of gas moles is equal on both sides (no shift), or when the pressure is raised by adding an inert gas at constant volume (no shift: the concentrations don't change).
  • Forgetting that pure solids and liquids don't count in the mole comparison.
  • Thinking the shift fully cancels the stress. It only partly offsets it.

Temperature and K

Raising T favors the endothermic direction, so K increases for an endothermic reaction (ΔH > 0) and decreases for an exothermic one. The van't Hoff relation makes this quantitative: ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁).
