Entropy & microstates

Why does a gas fill its container, and why does a hot cup of coffee cool down? Not because anything pulls: there are just overwhelmingly more ways for it to happen.

The idea

Shuffle a deck of cards and you will never get them back in perfect order. It isn't forbidden; it is just wildly unlikely, because there is only one ordered arrangement and an astronomical number of messy ones. Entropy (S) measures how many ways (microstates, W) the energy and particles of a system can be arranged: S = kB ln W. More ways means higher entropy, and systems drift toward the arrangements that are most numerous.

Entropy is larger for gases than liquids than solids (particles roam), for more moles of gas, for higher temperature (more ways to share energy), for larger or more complex molecules, and when substances mix or dissolve. The second law says the total entropy of the universe never decreases in a spontaneous process. A system can lose entropy (like water freezing) only if it dumps enough heat to the surroundings to raise their entropy by more.

Assumption upgrade Before, we thought reactions run downhill in energy only (exothermic = spontaneous). Now we see energy isn't the whole story: some endothermic processes (ice melting, salts dissolving) are spontaneous because they increase entropy. Next, Gibbs free energy combines both.

Predict first, then check.

  1. Open the partition with 4 particles, then with 40. Which one ever returns to "all on the left"?

    What you should see

    With 4 particles it happens now and then (1 in 16). With 40 it never will: the chance is (½)⁴⁰, about 1 in a trillion. With a mole of gas, it is impossible for all practical purposes. That is the second law.

  2. Look at the graph of arrangements. Which split has the most arrangements?

    What you should see

    The even split. There are far more ways to have about half on each side than all on one side, so the particles spend nearly all their time near the middle.

  3. Step through the reaction CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Predict the sign of ΔS first.

    What you should see

    Positive: a solid makes a solid plus a free gas. One mole of gas appears from nothing, so ΔS° = +160.6 J/mol·K.

Worked example

The Haber process makes ammonia for fertilizer: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g). Calculate ΔS° using S° (J/mol·K): N₂ 191.6, H₂ 130.6, NH₃ 192.5.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Predict the sign. 4 mol of gas become 2 mol of gas, so there is less disorder: ΔS should be negative.
  2. Products. ΣnS°(products) = 2 × 192.5 = 385.0 J/K.
  3. Reactants. ΣnS°(reactants) = 191.6 + 3 × 130.6 = 191.6 + 391.8 = 583.4 J/K.
  4. Subtract. ΔS° = 385.0 − 583.4 = −198.4 J/K (per mole of reaction as written). Negative, as predicted. Note the units: J/K, not kJ.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "entropy means messiness" and stopping there. Count the available arrangements of particles and energy.
  • Giving ΔS in kJ with ΔH in kJ: S is usually in J/K, ΔH in kJ. Convert before combining.
  • Forgetting the coefficient: ΔS° = ΣnS°(products) − ΣnS°(reactants).
  • Assuming the entropy of an element in its standard state is zero. (That is ΔH°f; elements have positive absolute S°. Only a perfect crystal at 0 K has S = 0.)
  • Thinking the entropy of a system can never decrease. It can, if the surroundings gain more than the system loses.

Quick rules for the sign of ΔS

Gas formed or more moles of gas: ΔS > 0. Fewer moles of gas: ΔS < 0. Melting, vaporizing, dissolving a solid: ΔS > 0 (usually). Freezing, condensing, precipitating: ΔS < 0. Heating a substance: ΔS > 0. The third law sets the zero: a perfect crystal at 0 K has S = 0, so every substance has a definite absolute entropy S° at 298 K.
