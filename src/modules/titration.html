Titration curves & indicators

Add base drop by drop to an acid and watch the pH. The curve tells you the concentration, the strength of the acid, and which indicator to use.

The idea

In a titration you add a solution of known concentration (the titrant) from a burette until the reaction is exactly complete, the equivalence point: moles of base added equal moles of acid originally present. From the volume you can work out the unknown concentration. An indicator changes color near the pH of the equivalence point to signal the endpoint.

The shape of the curve depends on the acid. Strong acid + strong base: the pH stays low, shoots up through 7 at the equivalence point, and levels off. Weak acid + strong base: the start is higher, a gentle buffer region follows (the pH barely moves because HA and A⁻ are both present), and the equivalence point is above 7 because A⁻ is a weak base. At half-equivalence exactly half the HA has become A⁻, so [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa.

Assumption upgrade Before, the equivalence point was always at pH 7 (strong acid and strong base). Now we know it depends on what is left in the flask: a weak acid's conjugate base makes the equivalence point basic, and a weak base's conjugate acid makes it acidic. We need an indicator that changes color near that pH.

Try this

Predict the curve, then drip.

  1. Titrate acetic acid and look at the half-equivalence point. What is the pH there?

    What you should see

    pH = 4.74 = pKa of acetic acid (Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵). Half the acid has turned into acetate, so [HA] = [A⁻] and the ratio in Henderson–Hasselbalch is 1.

  2. Titrate HCl with NaOH, then pick the wrong indicator for acetic acid (methyl orange).

    What you should see

    The equivalence point of acetic acid is near pH 8.7, but methyl orange has already changed color around pH 3 to 4.4, long before. You'd stop far too early. Phenolphthalein (8.2–10) is the right choice.

  3. Titrate HCN (Ka = 4.9×10⁻¹⁰). Is there a sharp jump?

    What you should see

    Hardly any: a very weak acid gives only a small, rounded rise at equivalence, so no ordinary indicator marks it well. Titrations work best when Ka is not too small.

Worked example

Vinegar is too concentrated to titrate directly, so 12.0 mL of it is diluted to 100.0 mL, giving about 0.100 M acetic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵). 25.00 mL of the diluted vinegar needs 25.00 mL of 0.100 M NaOH to reach the endpoint. Find (a) the pH after adding 10.00 mL, (b) the pH at equivalence, and (c) the molarity and percent acetic acid of the original vinegar.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Moles of acid. n(HA) = 0.02500 × 0.100 = 2.50 mmol. The base is 1:1, so V = 2.50 mmol / 0.100 M = 25.00 mL.
  2. (a) Before equivalence: a buffer. OH⁻ added = 10.00 × 0.100 = 1.00 mmol, which converts 1.00 mmol HA into A⁻: HA = 1.50 mmol, A⁻ = 1.00 mmol. pH = pKa + log(A⁻/HA) = 4.745 + log(1.00/1.50) = 4.745 − 0.176 = 4.57.
  3. (b) At equivalence: only A⁻. [A⁻] = 2.50 mmol / 50.0 mL = 0.0500 M. Kb = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.8×10⁻⁵ = 5.6×10⁻¹⁰, so [OH⁻] = √(5.6×10⁻¹⁰ × 0.0500) = 5.3×10⁻⁶ M.
  4. Convert. pOH = 5.28, so pH = 14 − 5.28 = 8.72. Above 7, as expected for a weak acid; phenolphthalein is a good indicator.
  5. (c) Back to the bottle. The diluted vinegar is 2.50 mmol / 25.00 mL = 0.100 M. It was diluted from 12.0 mL to 100.0 mL, so the original is 0.100 × (100.0/12.0) = 0.833 M. That is 0.833 × 60.05 = 50.0 g of acetic acid per liter: 5.0%, just what the label says.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the equivalence point is always pH 7. Only strong-acid/strong-base titrations are neutral there.
  • Using Henderson–Hasselbalch at the equivalence point or after it. It applies only in the buffer region, where both HA and A⁻ are present in comparable amounts.
  • Forgetting that the total volume increases as titrant is added when you compute concentrations.
  • Using volumes where moles are needed. Always compute moles (mmol works well), then divide by total volume.
  • Mixing up the equivalence point (moles equal) and the endpoint (indicator color change). A good indicator makes them very close.

Four regions of a weak-acid curve

(1) Start: only HA; pH from Ka. (2) Buffer region: HA and A⁻; use Henderson–Hasselbalch with moles. At half-equivalence pH = pKa. (3) Equivalence: only A⁻; pH from Kb of A⁻. (4) Past equivalence: excess OH⁻ controls the pH, and the weak base contributes almost nothing.
