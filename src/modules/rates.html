Reaction rates & rate laws

How fast does a reaction go, and what does the speed depend on? The rate law is the recipe that answers it.

The idea

A reaction rate is how quickly reactants disappear or products appear: change in concentration per second. Think of a busy dance floor where pairs must bump into each other to react. Crowd the floor and bumps happen far more often. Whether the rate doubles, quadruples or doesn't change when you double a concentration tells you the order with respect to that reactant.

A rate law has the form rate = k[A]m[B]n. The exponents m and n are the orders and must be found from experiments, not read from the balanced equation. The constant k is the rate constant: bigger k, faster reaction at the same concentrations.

Assumption upgrade Before, a balanced equation told us how much reacts. We assumed it also told us how fast. It doesn't: the coefficients give amounts, but the orders in the rate law come from experiment.

Initial-rates detective

Compare two trials where only one concentration changes. By what factor did the rate change?

Try this

Predict first, then watch the counts and the reaction count.

  1. First order: double the number of A particles. Does the reaction rate double?

    What you should see

    About twice as many reactions per second at 60 particles as at 30. Each A reacts on its own, so more A means proportionally more reactions: rate = k[A].

  2. Second order: do the same experiment. What happens now?

    What you should see

    Doubling A roughly quadruples the reactions per second, because every A can bump into twice as many partners and there are twice as many A. Rate = k[A]².

  3. Use the detective: find a pair of trials where only [B] changes.

    What you should see

    Divide the rates of those two trials, then ask: what power of the concentration ratio gives that factor? Ratio 1 means order 0, ratio equal to the concentration ratio means 1, its square means 2.

Worked example

Experiments for A + B → products: Trial 1: [A] = 0.10 M, [B] = 0.10 M, rate = 2.0×10⁻³ M/s. Trial 2: [A] = 0.20, [B] = 0.10, rate = 8.0×10⁻³. Trial 3: [A] = 0.10, [B] = 0.30, rate = 6.0×10⁻³. Find the rate law and k.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Order in A. Compare trials 1 and 2: only [A] changed, by a factor 2. The rate rose by 8.0/2.0 = 4. Since 2m = 4, m = 2.
  2. Order in B. Compare trials 1 and 3: only [B] changed, by a factor 3. The rate rose 6.0/2.0 = 3, and 3n = 3, so n = 1.
  3. Write the rate law. rate = k[A]²[B].
  4. Solve for k with any trial: k = rate / ([A]²[B]) = (2.0×10⁻³) / (0.10² × 0.10) = 2.0 M⁻²s⁻¹. The units come out of making M/s on the left: M⁻² s⁻¹ × M³ = M/s.

Common mistakes

  • Using the coefficients in the balanced equation as the orders. Only a (one-step) elementary reaction works that way.
  • Comparing two trials where more than one concentration changed. Change one at a time, or untangle the factors carefully.
  • Forgetting that k depends on temperature: a different temperature means a different k.
  • Dropping the units of k. They depend on the overall order: M/s for zero order, s⁻¹ for first, M⁻¹s⁻¹ for second.
  • Zero order does not mean zero rate. It means the rate doesn't depend on that concentration.

Units of k

Rate has units M/s. For overall order N, k has units M1−N·s⁻¹: zero order M/s, first order s⁻¹, second order M⁻¹s⁻¹, third order M⁻²s⁻¹. You can read the overall order straight from the units of k.
