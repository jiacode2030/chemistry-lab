Heat, temperature & phase changes

Heat is energy moving because of a temperature difference. Temperature is how fast the particles are jiggling. They are not the same thing, and a heating curve shows why.

The idea

Pour hot soup into a cold bowl: energy flows from hot to cold until the temperatures match. That energy flow is heat (q). Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. When you add heat to a solid, the particles jiggle harder and the temperature rises: q = mcΔT, where c is the specific heat capacity, the energy needed to raise 1 g by 1 °C.

But watch the heating curve: while ice melts, you keep adding heat and the temperature stays put at 0 °C. The energy isn't making particles faster; it is pulling them apart from their neighbors (breaking attractions). That energy is the enthalpy of fusion; for boiling, the enthalpy of vaporization. A plateau means "energy going into potential energy, not kinetic".

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed that adding heat always raises the temperature. Now we know that during a phase change heat goes into overcoming attractions, so the temperature stays constant and we need q = mΔH instead of q = mcΔT.

    What you should see

    The temperature stays at 0 °C while the ice melts: the particles leave their lattice sites one by one. The energy raises potential energy, not kinetic energy.

  2. Compare the two plateaus. Why is the boiling plateau so much longer?

    What you should see

    Boiling needs about 7 times as much energy per gram as melting (2260 J/g vs 334 J/g): the particles must be separated completely, not just loosened.

  3. Double the mass. What changes on the graph?

    What you should see

    The shape is the same but every segment is twice as long (twice as much energy for the same temperature change). The plateau temperatures don't change.

Worked example

How much heat is needed to turn 25.0 g of ice at −10.0 °C into water at 20.0 °C? (cice = 2.09 J/g·°C, cwater = 4.184 J/g·°C, ΔHfus = 334 J/g)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Split the path. Warm the ice to 0 °C, melt it at 0 °C, then warm the water to 20 °C. Three pieces; add them.
  2. Warm the ice. q₁ = mcΔT = (25.0)(2.09)(10.0) = 522.5 J.
  3. Melt it. No temperature change, so use q₂ = mΔHfus = (25.0)(334) = 8350 J.
  4. Warm the water. q₃ = (25.0)(4.184)(20.0) = 2092 J. Total = 522.5 + 8350 + 2092 = 10 964.5 J ≈ 1.10×10⁴ J (11.0 kJ). The melting step is by far the biggest share.

Common mistakes

  • Using q = mcΔT during a phase change. ΔT is zero there, but the heat isn't.
  • Forgetting a step: crossing several phases means several separate calculations, added up.
  • Mixing the specific heat of water with that of ice or steam. Each phase has its own c.
  • Using °C and K inconsistently. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, so ΔT is the same in both.
  • Saying "heat" and "temperature" mean the same. Heat is energy transferred; temperature is average kinetic energy.

Why the plateau? A particle view

Temperature tracks average kinetic energy. During melting the added energy loosens the lattice: the particles' potential energy rises while their kinetic energy (temperature) does not. Once every particle is free, further heat makes them move faster again. The attractions between particles are why ΔHvap is large for water: hydrogen bonds must be fully broken.

Thermal equilibrium

When two objects at different temperatures touch, heat flows from the hotter to the colder one until they reach the same temperature: thermal equilibrium. At the particle level, fast particles collide with slow ones and share kinetic energy until the average is equal. The total energy is conserved: the heat lost by one object equals the heat gained by the other. This is the idea behind every calorimetry problem in the next module.
