    Units & dimensional analysis

    Every chemistry calculation is a chain of conversions. Write the units next to the numbers, and let them cancel until only the unit you want is left.

    The idea

    A conversion factor is a fraction that equals 1, like 1000 mL / 1 L. Multiplying by 1 never changes the amount, only how it is written. Pick the version of the fraction that puts the unit you want to get rid of on the opposite side from where it sits now, and it cancels, the way a number cancels in a fraction.

    Nobody has to remember "multiply or divide?". If the units cancel to what you want, the setup is right. If they don't, the setup is wrong, before you touch a calculator.

    Unit-cancelling lab

    Tap a factor to add it to the chain. Tap ⇅ first to turn it upside down. Tap a factor in the chain to remove it.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Convert 2.50 L to mL. Add the factor that cancels L.

      What you should see

      Put mL on top and L on the bottom: 1000 mL / 1 L. The L cancels and leaves 2500 mL. If you add it upside down, L ends up squared on the bottom and mL is on the bottom too, and nothing cancels.

    2. Turn a rate into another rate: 72 km/h to m/s needs two factors.

      What you should see

      One factor changes km to m (1000 m / 1 km). The other changes h to s (1 h / 3600 s), flipped so that h cancels. The answer is 20 m/s.

    3. Go from grams to a count of particles. Which two conversions does it take?

      What you should see

      Grams to moles uses the molar mass (1 mol / 58.44 g). Moles to particles uses Avogadro's number. The gram units cancel first, then the moles.

    Worked example

    How many hydrogen atoms are in 5.0 g of water?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Start with what you have and what you want. You have 5.0 g H₂O. You want atoms of H.
    2. Grams to moles of water. The molar mass of H₂O is 18.02 g/mol, so multiply by (1 mol H₂O / 18.02 g H₂O). The grams cancel.
    3. Moles of water to moles of H. Each H₂O has 2 H, so multiply by (2 mol H / 1 mol H₂O). The moles of water cancel.
    4. Moles of H to atoms. Multiply by (6.022 × 10²³ atoms H / 1 mol H). The moles of H cancel, leaving atoms of H.
    5. Calculate. 5.0 × (1/18.02) × 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.3 × 10²³ atoms of H. Two significant figures, because 5.0 has two.

    Common mistakes

    • Multiplying by the factor the wrong way up. If the units don't cancel, flip it.
    • Dropping units partway through, so there is nothing to check the setup against.
    • Using a factor with mismatched labels, such as mol NaCl in one place and mol Na in another. They are different things and don't cancel.
    • Rounding in the middle of a chain. Keep a few extra digits and round once at the end.
    • Forgetting that squared or cubed units need squared or cubed factors: 1 m³ is (100 cm)³ = 10⁶ cm³, not 100 cm³.

    How to set up any conversion

    Write the starting quantity with its unit. Choose a factor with the unit you want to remove in the denominator. Cancel. Repeat until only the target unit remains. The numerical value of each factor comes from a definition (1 kg = 1000 g), a measured property (molar mass, density) or a chemical formula (2 H per H₂O).

    Exact definitions such as 1000 mL = 1 L never limit significant figures. Measured values such as a molar mass can. Molar masses are usually given to enough digits not to be the weakest link.

    Check yourself: to convert 3.2 km to m, which factor do you multiply by?

    Scientific notation & sig figs

    A measurement is only as good as its last trusted digit. Significant figures say how many digits you can trust. Scientific notation writes very big and very small numbers without a pile of zeros.

    The idea

    If a ruler is marked in millimeters and you read 12.3 cm, the 3 is your best guess between marks. Every digit you are sure of, plus that one guessed digit, is significant. Writing 12.30 claims you could read to the next digit; writing 12 claims you could not.

    Zeros are only placeholders when they sit before the first nonzero digit (0.0045), or at the end of a number with no decimal point (1200). A trailing zero after a decimal point (1.200) is a claim of precision, so it counts.

    2. Multiply 4.56 by 1.4. How many digits can you keep?

      What you should see

      The calculator shows 6.384, but 1.4 has only two significant figures, so the answer is 6.4. For multiplying and dividing, count significant figures and keep the fewest.

    3. Add 12.11 and 0.023. Which number sets the limit?

      What you should see

      The sum is 12.133, but 12.11 is only known to the hundredths column, so the answer is 12.13. For adding and subtracting, line up the decimal points and keep the fewest decimal places.

    Worked example

    A student weighs 2.50 g of a salt, then measures its volume as 1.2 mL. Report the density with the right number of significant figures.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Count significant figures. 2.50 has three (the trailing zero is after a decimal point). 1.2 has two.
    2. Pick the rule. Density is mass ÷ volume: a division, so keep the fewest significant figures. That is two.
    3. Calculate. 2.50 / 1.2 = 2.0833… on the calculator.
    4. Round once, at the end. Two significant figures: 2.1 g/mL.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting leading zeros: 0.0045 has two significant figures, not four.
    • Dropping a trailing zero that matters: 2.50 is not the same claim as 2.5.
    • Using the multiply rule for addition, or the other way round. Sig figs for × ÷; decimal places for + −.
    • Rounding in the middle of a multi-step problem. Round once, at the end.
    • Counting exact numbers (like 12 eggs, or 1000 mL = 1 L) as if they limited the answer. Exact numbers have unlimited significant figures.

    The two rounding rules

    Multiplying and dividing: the answer has as many significant figures as the input with the fewest. Adding and subtracting: the answer has as many decimal places as the input with the fewest. These are different because adding combines uncertainties in absolute terms (so what matters is the last column), while multiplying combines them in relative terms (so what matters is the count of digits).

    Scientific notation writes a number as m × 10n with 1 ≤ m < 10. Every digit in m is significant, so 6.022 × 10²³ has four. Moving the decimal point left raises the exponent; moving it right lowers it.

    Logs & pH math

    The concentration of H⁺ in water solutions spans fourteen powers of ten. A log scale squeezes that range into numbers from 0 to 14: that is all pH is.

    The idea

    Imagine a staircase where every step is ten times higher than the last. The step number tells you how many tens you have multiplied by, and that is what a logarithm is: log 1000 = 3 because 1000 = 10³. Going up one step on a log scale means multiplying by 10, not adding 1.

    pH is the step number for hydrogen ions, with a minus sign so that more acid gives a smaller number: pH = −log[H⁺]. A solution with pH 3 has ten times as many H⁺ as one with pH 4, and a hundred times as many as pH 5.

    Worked example

    What is the pH of 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M HCl? Then, a solution has pH 4.37. What is [H⁺]?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. HCl is a strong acid. It ionizes completely, so [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M.
    2. Take the log. log(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = log 3.2 + log 10⁻⁴ = 0.505 − 4 = −3.495.
    3. Flip the sign. pH = 3.495, which rounds to 3.49. Two significant figures in 3.2 give two decimal places.
    4. Now go backward. pH 4.37 means [H⁺] = 10−4.37. On the calculator, press 10x (or 2nd log) and enter −4.37.
    5. Answer. 10−4.37 = 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ M. Check: pH 4.37 is between 4 (10⁻⁴) and 5 (10⁻⁵), so [H⁺] should lie between them. It does.

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the minus sign: pH = −log[H⁺], and [H⁺] is a number less than 1, so its log is negative.
    • Thinking pH 3 is "a little more acidic" than pH 4. It is ten times more.
    • Reporting too many decimal places. The decimals in a pH equal the significant figures in the concentration.
    • Using ln instead of log. Chemistry's pH uses log base 10.
    • Going back with the wrong key: [H⁺] = 10−pH, not −10pH or log(pH).

    Why decimal places match significant figures

    The number before the decimal point in a log only says which power of ten you are at (the "characteristic"). Only the digits after the decimal point carry information about the precision of the measurement (the "mantissa"). So 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M (2 significant figures) gives pH = 3.49 (2 decimal places).

    Water autoionizes: [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Taking −log of both sides gives pH + pOH = 14.00. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M, so pH = 7.00: neutral.

    ICE-table algebra

    Equilibrium problems end in an equation with x in it. Sometimes x is tiny and can be ignored. Sometimes it can't. This module shows you how to tell.

    The idea

    An ICE table tracks Initial, Change and Equilibrium amounts. For a weak acid HA with starting concentration c, the change is "−x for HA, +x for H⁺ and A⁻", so the equilibrium expression becomes K = x²/(c − x).

    If K is tiny compared with c, then x is tiny compared with c, and c − x is almost just c. Then x ≈ √(Kc). The trick is to check that the shortcut was fair: if x turns out to be more than 5% of c, go back and solve the quadratic.

      Now x is more than 5% of c (about 35%). The dashed curve separates from the solid one at K and the shortcut is wrong by a large amount. Weaker solutions are more ionized, so the shortcut fails first for dilute solutions.

    3. Go the other way: a large K at moderate concentration.

      What you should see

      With K = 0.01 and c = 0.10, x is about 27% of c. The shortcut fails whenever K is not much smaller than c; a handy test is c/K < 100 (roughly).

    Worked example

    A 0.050 M solution of a weak acid HA has Ka = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴. Find [H⁺].

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. ICE table. HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Initial: 0.050, 0, 0. Change: −x, +x, +x. Equilibrium: 0.050 − x, x, x.
    2. Write K. Ka = x² / (0.050 − x) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴.
    3. Try the shortcut. x ≈ √(4.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.050) = √(2.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.5 × 10⁻³.
    4. Check. 4.5 × 10⁻³ / 0.050 = 9%, which is more than 5%. The shortcut is not allowed.
    5. Solve the quadratic. x² + (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)x − 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 0, so x = [−4.0 × 10⁻⁴ + √((4.0 × 10⁻⁴)² + 4(2.0 × 10⁻⁵))] / 2 = 4.3 × 10⁻³ M. That is [H⁺].

    Common mistakes

    • Using the shortcut without checking it. State the check on paper every time: x/c × 100 < 5%.
    • Checking against the wrong number. Compare x with the starting concentration of the species that is being subtracted from.
    • Rearranging the quadratic wrongly: x² + Kx − Kc = 0 (the sign of the Kc term is negative).
    • Keeping the negative root of the quadratic. A concentration cannot be negative.
    • Forgetting that the 5% rule is a rule of thumb; if x is 6% your answer is off by about 3%, not ruined.

    Setting up and solving

    Rearrange K = x²/(c − x) into a standard quadratic: x² + Kx − Kc = 0. Then x = [−K + √(K² + 4Kc)] / 2. Take only the positive root.

    A faster way to decide: if c/K > 400 the shortcut error is under about 5%, and the x-is-small assumption holds. The slider above lets you watch the boundary.

    Moles & molar mass

    Atoms are far too small to count one by one, so chemists count them by weighing. The mole is the bridge between a mass you can measure and a number of particles you can't see.

    The idea

    A grocer sells eggs by the dozen and rice by the kilogram, and has a rule that turns one into the other: "a dozen eggs weighs about 600 g". The chemist's dozen is the mole: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. The chemist's rule for turning a count into a mass is the molar mass: the mass of one mole, in grams. It is the number on the periodic table, with units of g/mol.

    The surprise is that one mole of different substances weighs very different amounts, because their particles have different masses. The same mass of different substances holds very different numbers of particles. Try both below.

      The beam is level because the masses are equal, but carbon has about 2 mol (24 ÷ 12.01) and water about 1.3 mol (24 ÷ 18.02). Lighter particles means more particles in the same mass.

    3. Glucose is a big molecule. How does 1 mol of glucose compare with 1 mol of water?

      What you should see

      180.16 g against 18.02 g: ten times the mass for the same number of molecules, because each glucose molecule is ten times as heavy.

    Worked example

    How many moles and how many molecules are in 9.00 g of water?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Find the molar mass. H₂O: 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol.
    2. Mass to moles. n = m / M = 9.00 g ÷ 18.02 g/mol = 0.499 mol.
    3. Moles to molecules. N = n × NA = 0.499 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
    4. Sense check. 9.00 g is half of 18.02 g, so half a mole and half of Avogadro's number. ✓

    Common mistakes

    • Thinking a mole is a mass. It is a number of particles; the molar mass is what connects it to grams.
    • Using the molar mass of an atom for a molecule. For H₂O add up both H and the O.
    • Dividing when you should multiply (or the reverse). Check that the grams cancel.
    • Mixing "mol of molecules" with "mol of atoms". One mole of H₂O molecules contains 2 mol of H atoms.
    • Reading 6.022 × 10²³ as a mass. It has units of particles per mole.

    The two conversions

    Mass ↔ moles: n = m / M, where M is the molar mass in g/mol. Moles ↔ particles: N = n NA, with NA = 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. Almost every calculation later in the course runs through moles, so moles are the hub and grams and particles sit at the two ends.

    The molar mass of a compound is the sum of the molar masses of its atoms, counting each as many times as it appears in the formula: for glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, it is 6(12.01) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.16 g/mol.

    Mass spectra & average atomic mass

    Atoms of the same element can have different masses. A mass spectrometer sorts them, and the number on the periodic table is the weighted average of what it finds.

    The idea

    Atoms of one element all have the same number of protons, but can have different numbers of neutrons. These versions are isotopes. Chlorine atoms come as ³⁵Cl (18 neutrons) and ³⁷Cl (20 neutrons). In a sample of chlorine, about three in four atoms are ³⁵Cl.

    A mass spectrometer ionizes atoms and sends them through a magnetic field. Lighter ions bend more, so the instrument sorts the beam by mass, and a detector counts each kind. The result is a mass spectrum: one peak per isotope, with the height showing how common it is. Average the masses with each weighted by its abundance and you get the atomic mass on the periodic table. That is why it isn't a whole number.

    Composition & empirical formulas

    Weigh the pieces of a compound and you can work out its formula. The trick is that formulas count atoms, and scales measure mass, so you have to convert through moles.

    The idea

    A fruit salad is 60 g apples and 30 g grapes. Is that 2 apples to 1 grape? Not at all, because an apple weighs far more than a grape. To find the count ratio you divide each mass by the mass of one piece. For atoms, that is the molar mass, so grams become moles.

    The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. The molecular formula is the real count in one molecule, which is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆, but its empirical formula is just CH₂O.

    From percent to formula

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Hydrogen peroxide is 5.93% H and 94.07% O by mass. Is the formula HO₁₆? Walk through the steps.

      What you should see

      In 100 g there is 5.88 mol H and 5.88 mol O: a 1 : 1 ratio, so HO is the empirical formula. Mass percent and atom count are not the same thing: oxygen is the bigger share of the mass but only equal in count.

    2. Iron(III) oxide gives a ratio of 1 : 1.5. What do you do next?

      What you should see

      You can't have 1.5 atoms, so multiply both by 2 to get the whole numbers 2 : 3, giving Fe₂O₃. Ratios ending in .5, .33 or .25 call for multiplying by 2, 3 or 4.

    3. Octane is 84.1% C and 15.9% H. How far do you have to multiply?

      What you should see

      The ratio is 1 : 2.25, so multiply by 4 to get 4 : 9: the empirical formula is C₄H₉. The actual molecular formula, C₈H₁₈, needs the molar mass to decide.

    Worked example

    A compound is 40.0% C, 6.71% H and 53.3% O by mass, and its molar mass is 180 g/mol. Find the empirical and molecular formulas.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Assume 100 g. The percentages become grams: 40.0 g C, 6.71 g H, 53.3 g O.
    2. Grams to moles. C: 40.0 / 12.01 = 3.33 mol. H: 6.71 / 1.008 = 6.66 mol. O: 53.3 / 16.00 = 3.33 mol.
    3. Divide by the smallest (3.33). C : H : O = 1.00 : 2.00 : 1.00. They are already whole numbers.
    4. Empirical formula. CH₂O, with a mass of 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol.
    5. Molecular formula. 180 / 30.03 = 6.0, so multiply everything by 6: C₆H₁₂O₆.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the mass ratio as if it were the atom ratio. Always convert grams to moles first.
    • Rounding 1.5 or 1.33 to 1 or 2. Multiply by 2 or 3 instead; only round values within about 0.1 of a whole number.
    • Giving the empirical formula when the question asks for the molecular formula (you need the molar mass).
    • Forgetting to divide by the smallest number of moles, not the largest.
    • Dividing the molar mass by the wrong thing. Use the molar mass of the empirical formula, not of one element.

    The recipe

    (1) Assume 100 g so percent becomes grams. (2) Convert each to moles. (3) Divide by the smallest. (4) If any value isn't close to a whole number, multiply all of them by the same small integer. (5) The whole numbers are subscripts in the empirical formula. (6) For the molecular formula, divide the molar mass by the empirical formula mass to get a whole number n, and multiply the subscripts by n.

    For an ionic compound such as Fe₂O₃, the empirical formula is the formula, because there are no separate molecules.

    Electron configuration & PES

    Electrons sit in shells and subshells, and a photoelectron spectrum is a photograph of that structure: one peak per subshell, as tall as the number of electrons in it.

    The idea

    Think of an apartment building. Each floor (shell) holds a limited number of people, and each floor is divided into rooms (subshells: s, p, d). Electrons fill the lowest-energy rooms first, one to a space before doubling up. That filling order gives the electron configuration, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ for oxygen.

    In photoelectron spectroscopy you shine high-energy light on atoms and measure how much energy it takes to knock each electron out. Electrons close to the nucleus are held tightly, so they need a lot of energy; outer electrons need little. Each peak is a subshell. Its position tells you the binding energy; its height tells you how many electrons share that energy.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Each peak is a subshell. The heights are the numbers of electrons: 2, 2, 6 and 1.
    2. Assign them in order. From the highest energy (the most tightly held electrons), the order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.
    3. Total the electrons. 2 + 2 + 6 + 1 = 11 electrons. A neutral atom has equal protons, so Z = 11.
    4. Answer. The element is sodium. The 1-electron peak at the lowest energy is the valence electron in a new shell.

    Common mistakes

    • Reading peak height as energy. Height is the number of electrons; position is the energy.
    • Forgetting that the axis runs backwards: the peak farthest left has the highest binding energy.
    • Pairing electrons in p orbitals before every box has one (Hund's rule says spread out first).
    • Thinking the 2s and 2p peaks are at the same energy. They are close, but 2p electrons are held a little less tightly.
    • Thinking binding energies stay put as Z grows. Every peak shifts to higher energy as the nucleus gains protons.

    Filling the boxes

    Three rules put electrons in boxes: Aufbau (fill the lowest-energy subshell first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d …), Pauli exclusion (a box holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins) and Hund's rule (in a set of equal-energy boxes, put one electron in each before pairing any).

    The capacity of a subshell is 2(2ℓ + 1): s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10. A shell n holds 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18 and so on. In a PES, the peak for a full subshell has relative height 2 (s), 6 (p) or 10 (d).

    Orbital shapes in 3D

    An orbital is not a track the electron follows. It is a map of where the electron is likely to be found. Take enough snapshots and the map appears.

    The idea

    Imagine photographing a bee near a hive a thousand times and drawing a dot for each photo. You never see its path, but the cloud of dots shows where it spends its time. The electron works like that, except that nobody can say where it is at any moment; quantum mechanics only gives the odds. Each orbital is a different pattern of odds.

    The pattern has a size and a shape. An s orbital is a ball. A p orbital has two lobes, like a dumbbell, pointing along x, y or z. A d orbital has four lobes (or a donut with two). Higher shells (n) are bigger, and can have nodes: places where the electron is never found.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Identify n and ℓ. 3p means n = 3 and ℓ = 1 (s = 0, p = 1, d = 2).
    2. Angular nodes. The number of angular nodes equals ℓ, so a 3p orbital has 1.
    3. Radial nodes. n − ℓ − 1 = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1.
    4. Count the orbitals in the shell. n = 3 has subshells 3s (1 orbital), 3p (3) and 3d (5): 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 orbitals, which is n². With two electrons per orbital, the shell holds 18.

    Common mistakes

    • Picturing the electron as a planet circling the nucleus on a flat track. An orbital is a 3D probability map.
    • Thinking the outline of an orbital is a wall. It is just the surface inside which the electron spends most (say 90%) of its time.
    • Thinking the p orbital's two lobes are two electrons. One electron (or a pair) is spread over the whole dumbbell.
    • Confusing the plus and minus signs of the lobes with electric charge. They are the sign of the wavefunction, which matters for bonding but isn't a charge.
    • Mixing up radial and angular nodes: radial nodes are spheres (n − ℓ − 1); angular nodes are planes or cones through the nucleus (ℓ).

    Quantum numbers in one place

    Three quantum numbers name an orbital. n (1, 2, 3 …) sets size and energy. ℓ (0 to n − 1) sets the shape: s, p, d, f. mℓ (from −ℓ to +ℓ) sets the orientation, so a subshell has 2ℓ + 1 orbitals. A fourth number, the spin ms (±½), tells which of the two electrons in an orbital you mean.

    Nodes tell you the shape without the picture: total nodes = n − 1; angular nodes = ℓ; radial nodes = n − ℓ − 1.

    Periodic trends & Coulomb's law

    Atomic size, ionization energy and electronegativity all come from one question: how hard does the nucleus pull on the outermost electron, and how far away is that electron?

    The idea

    Opposite charges attract, and the pull falls off fast with distance: Coulomb's law says the force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the distance squared. Double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter; double one charge and it doubles.

    An outer electron feels the nucleus's positive charge, but the inner (core) electrons cancel part of it. That leaves an effective nuclear charge, roughly the number of protons minus the number of core electrons. Moving across a period, protons are added but the electrons go into the same shell, so the pull grows and the atom shrinks. Moving down a group, a whole new shell is added, so the outer electron is much farther away and the atom grows.

    3. Compare the distance. Sodium's outer electron is in a larger shell, so it is farther from the nucleus than neon's.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying "more protons means a bigger pull" without noticing the core electrons cancel most of it when you move down a group.
    • Answering with only "it is farther away". A full answer names both the charge felt (Zeff) and the distance (shell).
    • Thinking atoms get bigger across a period because they have more electrons. The electrons go into the same shell and the stronger pull wins.
    • Forgetting the small dips: first ionization energy drops from Be to B and from N to O, because of subshell and electron-pairing effects.
    • Mixing up atomic radius (shrinks across) with ionic radius (cations are smaller than their atoms; anions are larger).

    Summary of the trends

    Across a period (left to right): Zeff goes up, atomic radius goes down, first ionization energy generally goes up, electronegativity goes up. Down a group: the outer electron is in a larger shell, so radius goes up, ionization energy goes down, and electronegativity goes down.

    Cations are smaller than their parent atoms (fewer electrons, and often a whole shell is gone, so the same nuclear pull is shared by fewer electrons). Anions are larger (more electrons repel each other while the nuclear pull is unchanged).

    Bonding & potential energy

    Atoms bond because two atoms together have lower energy than the same two atoms apart. The energy well between them explains bond length, bond strength and what kind of bond forms.

    The idea

    Bring two atoms together. At first the nucleus of each starts to attract the other atom's electrons, and the energy drops. Push them closer and the two positive nuclei begin to repel each other, and the electron clouds crowd, so the energy shoots up. In between there is a sweet spot with the lowest energy. That distance is the bond length, and the depth of the well is the bond energy, the energy you must supply to pull the atoms apart.

    What kind of bond forms depends on how the atoms share electrons. Two similar atoms share evenly (nonpolar covalent). A pull from one atom's higher electronegativity makes the sharing uneven (polar covalent). If a metal meets a nonmetal, the electron is taken completely and the oppositely charged ions attract (ionic). Metals share their outer electrons with all their neighbors in a "sea" (metallic).

    3. NaCl. A metal with a nonmetal. ΔEN = 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23. Sodium gives its outer electron to chlorine: ionic, Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

    Common mistakes

    • Thinking bond breaking releases energy. Breaking a bond always costs energy; forming one releases it.
    • Mixing up bond length (where the minimum is) with bond energy (how deep it is). Stronger bonds are usually shorter.
    • Calling H–Cl ionic because it is "polar". It is polar covalent: the electrons are shared, just unevenly.
    • Believing there is a sharp cutoff between types. Real bonds lie along a continuum; the cutoffs are conventions.
    • Forgetting that two metals bond metallically, not covalently or ionically.

    Reading the curve

    On a plot of potential energy against the distance between two nuclei, zero is the energy of the two separated atoms. The curve dips below zero as the atoms attract, reaches a minimum at the bond length, and rises steeply as they are pushed closer. The depth from zero down to the minimum is the bond energy (the energy needed to separate the atoms completely).

    Coulomb's law is behind all of it: attraction between nuclei and the other atom's electrons, repulsion between nuclei, and repulsion between electron clouds. The minimum is where these balance. Ionic bonds follow the same logic: the higher the charges and the smaller the ions, the stronger the attraction (lattice energy).

    Lewis structures & formal charge

    A Lewis structure is a bookkeeping diagram for valence electrons: who shares, who keeps, and who has a full shell. Formal charge tells you which of several possible diagrams is the most believable.

    The idea

    Atoms are happiest with a full outer shell: two electrons for hydrogen, eight for the elements of periods 2 and 3 (the octet rule). They get there by sharing electron pairs in bonds. Every electron in your count has to land somewhere: in a bond (shared between two atoms) or in a lone pair (kept by one atom).

    Sometimes more than one diagram works. To choose, give every atom a formal charge: the electrons it brought (its valence electrons) minus the electrons it "owns" in the diagram (all its lone-pair electrons, plus half of its bonding electrons). The best structure has formal charges as close to zero as possible, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Count valence electrons. H 1 + C 4 + N 5 = 10 electrons.
    2. Skeleton. Carbon is the least electronegative atom that can make several bonds, so it goes in the middle. H hangs off only one bond: H–C–N. That uses 4 electrons (2 bonds).
    3. Add lone pairs. 6 electrons remain. Give nitrogen three lone pairs (6 electrons). Now N has 8 electrons around it, but carbon has only 4 and H has 2.
    4. Make multiple bonds. Move two of nitrogen's lone pairs into the bond between C and N: H–C≡N. Now C has 8 (4 bonds), N has 8 (3 bonds and 1 lone pair) and H has 2.
    5. Formal charges. H: 1 − 0 − 1 = 0. C: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0. N: 5 − 2 − 3 = 0. All zero: the best structure, and the sum is 0, as it must be for a neutral molecule.

    Common mistakes

    • Miscounting electrons for ions: add one for each negative charge, subtract one for each positive charge.
    • Counting only bonds when computing formal charge. You must count lone-pair electrons and half the bonding electrons.
    • Giving hydrogen more than one bond. Hydrogen holds only two electrons, so it can never be central.
    • Forgetting that resonance forms are not separate molecules that flip back and forth. The real structure is the hybrid.
    • Ignoring the check that the formal charges must add up to the overall charge of the species.

    The recipe

    (1) Add up the valence electrons (adjust for charge). (2) Put the least electronegative atom (not H) in the middle and join the others to it with single bonds. (3) Finish the octets of the outer atoms with lone pairs. (4) Put any electrons left on the central atom. (5) If the central atom lacks an octet, turn lone pairs from outer atoms into double or triple bonds. (6) Calculate formal charges and, if there is a choice, pick the structure with the smallest ones.

    Not every molecule follows the octet. Boron compounds like BF₃ are happy with six electrons; some atoms in period 3 or lower (like S in SO₂ or SF₆) can hold more than eight. The formal-charge test can favor an expanded octet (as with SO₂), but the course often asks you to draw the octet structure; follow your teacher's convention.

    VSEPR shapes & polarity

    Electron clouds repel each other, so they spread out as far as they can in three dimensions. The shape of a molecule is the arrangement of the atoms sitting in that spread-out cloud.

    The idea

    Tie two balloons together by their knots and they point in opposite directions. Tie three and they make a flat triangle. Tie four and they form a pyramid with a triangular base, a tetrahedron. Nothing holds them in that shape except that each balloon pushes on the others.

    Around a central atom, every region of electron density is a balloon: a bond (single, double or triple, they all count as one region) or a lone pair. Negative charges repel, so the regions spread out. Then you name the shape using only the atoms, because the lone pairs are invisible in the final shape but they still take up room.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting only bonded atoms. Water has four regions around oxygen (two bonds, two lone pairs), not two.
    • Counting a double or triple bond as two or three regions. Any multiple bond is one region.
    • Naming the shape from the electron geometry. Water's electron geometry is tetrahedral; its shape is bent.
    • Thinking polar bonds always make a polar molecule. CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds and are nonpolar, because the dipoles cancel.
    • Thinking a symmetric-looking shape guarantees nonpolar. The outer atoms must also be identical; CH₂Cl₂ is polar.
    • Forgetting that lone pairs squeeze bond angles: 109.5° → 107° (one lone pair) → 104.5° (two).

    Hybridization, sigma & pi bonds

    Atoms can blend their own orbitals into new ones that point exactly where the bonds go. Bonds then form by overlap, end to end (sigma) or side by side (pi), and that explains why double bonds are rigid.

    The idea

    A carbon atom has one s and three p orbitals in its outer shell, but methane has four identical C–H bonds 109.5° apart. The p orbitals alone point 90° apart and the s orbital points nowhere, so none of them fit. Carbon hybridizes: it mixes the s and the three p orbitals into four identical sp³ hybrids that point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Like mixing paint, you get new colors with the same total amount.

    The rule of thumb: count the regions of electron density (the same count as in VSEPR). Two regions: sp (180°). Three: sp² (120°). Four: sp³ (109.5°). Hybrid orbitals make σ (sigma) bonds, overlapping head-on along the line between the nuclei. Any p orbital left over can overlap sideways above and below the bond to make a π (pi) bond. A double bond is one σ plus one π; a triple bond is one σ plus two π.

      Three sp² hybrids in a flat triangle (120°) and one p orbital left over, standing perpendicular to the plane. That p orbital is the one that makes π bonds in ethene or formaldehyde.

    3. Twist the C=C bond in ethene to 90°.

      What you should see

      At 90° the two p orbitals no longer overlap sideways, the π bond is broken and the energy cost is large (about 270 kJ/mol). Double bonds are rigid, which is why cis and trans isomers exist. Compare ethane, where twisting costs only 12 kJ/mol.

    Worked example

    For formaldehyde, H₂C=O, find the hybridization of carbon and count the σ and π bonds.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Lewis structure. C is bonded to two H atoms and double bonded to O: H–C(=O)–H. Carbon has no lone pairs.
    2. Count regions around C. Two single bonds and one double bond (one region): 3 regions.
    3. Hybridization. Three regions give sp² (120°, trigonal planar), leaving one unhybridized p orbital on carbon.
    4. Count bonds. Each single bond is one σ: 2 C–H σ bonds. The double bond is one σ plus one π, so in total 3 σ bonds and 1 π bond.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting a double bond as two regions of electron density. It is one region: one σ plus one π.
    • Thinking hybrid orbitals are a property of molecules instead of atoms. Each atom in the molecule has its own hybridization.
    • Saying a lone pair does not count. It occupies a hybrid orbital: nitrogen in NH₃ (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) is sp³.
    • Forgetting that π bonds come only from unhybridized p orbitals. sp² carbon has one, sp carbon has two.
    • Believing π bonds are stronger than σ bonds. Head-on overlap is better: a σ bond is stronger than a π bond in the same pair of atoms.

    Counting hybrids

    The number of hybrid orbitals always equals the number of orbitals you mixed: 1 s + 3 p → 4 sp³; 1 s + 2 p → 3 sp²; 1 s + 1 p → 2 sp. The hybrids hold both bonding pairs and lone pairs. An atom in a molecule with 5 or 6 regions needs d orbitals (sp³d, sp³d²), an idea that goes beyond the course's usual cases.

    Single bond: 1 σ. Double: 1 σ + 1 π. Triple: 1 σ + 2 π. So the number of σ bonds equals the number of bonded pairs of atoms, and the number of π bonds equals the total bond order minus the number of bonded pairs.

    AP Unit 3

    Intermolecular forces

    Molecules attract each other without bonding. How strongly decides whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature, and how hot it must get to boil.

    The idea

    Inside a molecule, covalent bonds hold atoms together. Between molecules, much weaker intermolecular forces (IMFs) act like faint Velcro. To boil a liquid you do not break any bonds in the molecules: you only pull the molecules away from each other. So the stronger the Velcro, the more heat is needed, and the higher the boiling point.

    There are three kinds, all from Coulomb's law. London dispersion forces exist in every substance: electrons slosh around and make momentary dipoles; bigger electron clouds slosh more, so larger molecules attract more. Dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding is an especially strong dipole–dipole force when H is bonded to N, O or F and points at a lone pair on another N, O or F.

      At 200 K argon (boils at 87 K) is a gas of separate atoms, while water (boils at 373 K) is held together as a solid or liquid clump. Same temperature, very different attractions.

      Butane has many more electrons, so a bigger, more polarizable cloud and stronger dispersion forces. It is still a clump at 150 K when methane (boiling at 112 K) has flown apart.

    Worked example

    Rank CH₄, CH₃OH and CH₃Cl from lowest to highest boiling point, and name the strongest IMF in each.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. CH₄. Nonpolar, so only London dispersion forces, and a small molecule: the weakest attraction.
    2. CH₃Cl. Polar (the C–Cl bond is polar and the shape is not symmetric), so dipole–dipole forces plus dispersion. Stronger than CH₄.
    3. CH₃OH. Has an O–H bond, so it can hydrogen bond with other molecules: the strongest IMF of the three.
    4. Ranking. CH₄ (112 K) < CH₃Cl (249 K) < CH₃OH (338 K), the order of increasing IMF strength.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying boiling breaks the bonds inside the molecule. Boiling water gives H₂O vapor, not hydrogen and oxygen.
    • Calling any molecule with H a hydrogen-bonder. The H must be bonded to N, O or F (CH₄ and HCl do not).
    • Forgetting that dispersion forces exist in every substance, and dominate in large nonpolar molecules.
    • Mixing up intermolecular forces with covalent bond strength: the covalent bonds in water are far stronger than the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.
    • Comparing boiling points without considering size: a large nonpolar molecule can beat a small polar one.

    Ranking by strength

    For molecules of similar size, the order is: hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion. For molecules of very different sizes, dispersion forces can win, so compare molar masses too. Ionic substances (full charges) are held by even stronger forces, and in a solution an ion is attracted to the partial charges on water (ion–dipole).

    These attractions also explain surface tension, viscosity, vapor pressure and solubility. Stronger IMFs mean higher boiling point, higher viscosity, higher surface tension and lower vapor pressure at a given temperature.

    AP Unit 33D

    Types of solids

    Every solid is a pattern of particles, but what the particles are and what holds them decides everything: how hard it is, whether it conducts, and when it melts.

    The idea

    Ask two questions: what are the particles, and what holds them together? Table salt is ions held by the pull between opposite charges. Copper is metal cations bathed in shared electrons. Diamond is atoms bonded to each other in one giant network. Dry ice is separate molecules held by weak intermolecular forces.

    Each answer predicts behavior. If the particles are charged ions locked in place, the solid won't conduct, but it will when melted, and it shatters if you shift a layer. If electrons roam, it conducts and bends. If every atom is covalently locked in place, it is very hard and melts only at extreme heat. If the forces are weak, it is soft and melts easily.

    2. Conducts when molten but not as a solid. Charged particles that are free to move only after melting: ions. Network solids do not conduct even when molten (there are no charges to move).
    3. Answer. An ionic solid (like NaCl, mp 801 °C). In the solid, the ions are locked in the lattice; when melted, they can move and carry current.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying ionic solids conduct because they contain ions. In the solid the ions can't move; they conduct only when molten or dissolved.
    • Thinking molecular solids contain no covalent bonds. They do, inside each molecule; it is the attractions between molecules that are weak.
    • Calling a metal's bonding ionic. Metal atoms share a sea of delocalized electrons.
    • Believing network solids melt like molecular ones. Melting a network solid means breaking covalent bonds.
    • Explaining ionic brittleness without mentioning that sliding a layer brings like charges together.

    Lattice energy

    For ionic solids the strength of the lattice follows Coulomb's law: higher charges and smaller ions give stronger attraction and a higher melting point. MgO (2+ and 2−, mp 2852 °C) melts far above NaCl (1+ and 1−, mp 801 °C). The same logic orders NaCl above KCl, because the sodium ion is smaller.

    Metals owe their luster, conductivity and malleability to delocalized electrons. Alloys mix metals: in a substitutional alloy (brass) atoms of similar size replace each other; in an interstitial alloy (steel) small atoms like carbon sit in the gaps, making the layers harder to slide.

    AP Unit 3

    Gas laws & kinetic theory

    A gas is mostly empty space with tiny particles flying around and bouncing off the walls. Pressure, volume, temperature and amount are four ways of describing the same crowd.

    The idea

    Pressure is the combined push of billions of particles hitting a wall. Heat the gas and the particles move faster, so they hit harder and more often. Squeeze the same particles into a smaller space and they hit the walls more often. Add more particles and there are more hits. That story is the kinetic molecular theory, and the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, is its summary.

    Temperature has a precise meaning here: it is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. At the same temperature, light molecules move faster than heavy ones, but all have the same average kinetic energy. Individual speeds vary widely, following the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

      At 0.5 L the real pressure is about 39 atm against 49 atm for an ideal gas (Z ≈ 0.8): the attractions between molecules make the pressure lower. Squeeze much further (try 0.06 L) and the molecules' own volume takes over, so the real pressure is far higher than ideal.

      What you should see

      Light helium molecules are fastest and its curve is broad; heavy CO₂ molecules are slower with a narrow curve. All three have the same average kinetic energy, because it depends only on temperature.

    Worked example

    A 5.00 L tank holds 0.250 mol of nitrogen at 300. K. What is the pressure? If the gas is heated to 450. K in the same tank, what is the new pressure?

    1. Write the ideal gas law. PV = nRT, so P = nRT/V. With atm and L, use R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).
    2. Substitute. P = (0.250)(0.08206)(300.) / 5.00 = 1.23 atm.
    3. Constant volume and amount. P/T stays constant, so P2 = P1(T2/T1) = 1.23 × (450/300) = 1.85 atm.
    4. Check. The temperature went up by a factor of 1.5, and so did the pressure. ✓ Always use kelvins.

    Common mistakes

    • Using °C in gas-law problems. Always convert to kelvins: K = °C + 273.15.
    • Using the wrong value of R for the units: 0.08206 for atm and L, 8.314 for joules, 62.36 for torr and L.
    • Thinking heavier gases have more kinetic energy at the same temperature. The average KE is the same; the heavier ones are just slower.
    • Thinking all particles move at the average speed. The speeds span a wide range.
    • Expecting ideal behavior at high pressure and low temperature, where real gases deviate.

    Deviations from ideal

    The ideal gas law assumes particles occupy no volume and attract nothing. At high pressure the particles are crowded, their own volume matters and the pressure is higher than ideal. At low temperature the attractions pull particles together, so they hit the walls less hard and the pressure is lower than ideal. The van der Waals equation corrects for both: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. Gases with stronger IMFs (large a) and larger molecules (large b) deviate more.

    Dalton's law: in a mixture, each gas contributes its own partial pressure, PA = XAPtotal, where XA is the mole fraction. The root-mean-square speed is urms = √(3RT/M) with M in kg/mol.
