Calorimetry

You can't see energy, but you can see a thermometer. Calorimetry measures heat by watching how much a known amount of water warms up or cools down.

The idea

Energy is never lost: whatever one object gives up, another gains. Drop a hot metal into cool water. The metal cools, the water warms, and they settle at one temperature. The heat the metal loses equals the heat the water gains: qlost + qgained = 0. Since q = mcΔT for each, you can solve for the final temperature, or for an unknown specific heat.

A reaction works the same way. If it releases heat, the solution around it warms up (exothermic, ΔH negative); if it absorbs heat, the solution gets cold (endothermic, ΔH positive). The water gained what the reaction lost, so qrxn = −qsolution. That is how a hot pack and a cold pack differ.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed all the heat stays between the two objects. A real cup absorbs and leaks some. We ignore the cup for now, but a bomb calorimeter's heat capacity C (kJ/°C) is exactly how we account for it.

Try this

Predict the final temperature, then drop the metal in.

  1. Lead versus aluminum at the same mass and temperature. Which warms the water more?

    What you should see

    Aluminum, because it has a much larger specific heat (0.897 vs 0.129 J/g·°C): each gram holds about seven times as much heat. Lead's temperature drops a lot but it carries little energy.

  2. Use a lot more water. What happens to the final temperature?

    What you should see

    The final temperature ends closer to the water's starting temperature: a large mass of water changes temperature only a little for the same heat.

  3. Dissolve ammonium nitrate. Is it a hot pack or a cold pack?

    What you should see

    Cold. Dissolving NH₄NO₃ absorbs heat from the water (endothermic, ΔH positive). Instant cold packs use this.

Worked example

A 50.0 g piece of metal at 99.0 °C is dropped into 100.0 g of water at 21.0 °C. The final temperature is 24.0 °C. What is the specific heat of the metal? (cwater = 4.184 J/g·°C)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Heat gained by the water. q = mcΔT = (100.0)(4.184)(24.0 − 21.0) = 1255 J.
  2. The metal lost the same amount. qmetal = −1255 J (negative because it lost heat).
  3. Solve for c. −1255 = (50.0)(c)(24.0 − 99.0) = (50.0)(c)(−75.0), so c = 1255 / 3750 = 0.335 J/g·°C.
  4. Check. The metal's ΔT is large and the water's is small, so the metal's c should be much less than water's. 0.335 ≪ 4.184 ✓.

Common mistakes

  • Getting the sign wrong: the water's q is positive when it warms, the metal's q is negative; they add to zero.
  • Using ΔT = Tinitial − Tfinal. Always final minus initial.
  • Using the mass of the metal with the specific heat of water (or the reverse).
  • Forgetting that qrxn = −qsolution, and that ΔH is per mole, so divide by moles.
  • Forgetting that the solution's mass includes the dissolved solid.

Coffee-cup and bomb calorimeters

A coffee-cup calorimeter is open to the air, so it measures heat at constant pressure: q = ΔH. A bomb calorimeter is a sealed steel container in a water bath, so it measures heat at constant volume, and it comes with a calibrated heat capacity C (in kJ/°C): q = CΔT. In both cases the heat absorbed by the surroundings has the opposite sign of the heat released by the reaction.
