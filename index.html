    Math toolkit

    Units & dimensional analysis

    Every chemistry calculation is a chain of conversions. Write the units next to the numbers, and let them cancel until only the unit you want is left.

    The idea

    A conversion factor is a fraction that equals 1, like 1000 mL / 1 L. Multiplying by 1 never changes the amount, only how it is written. Pick the version of the fraction that puts the unit you want to get rid of on the opposite side from where it sits now, and it cancels, the way a number cancels in a fraction.

    Nobody has to remember "multiply or divide?". If the units cancel to what you want, the setup is right. If they don't, the setup is wrong, before you touch a calculator.

    Unit-cancelling lab

    Tap a factor to add it to the chain. Tap ⇅ first to turn it upside down. Tap a factor in the chain to remove it.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. Convert 2.50 L to mL. Add the factor that cancels L.

      What you should see

      Put mL on top and L on the bottom: 1000 mL / 1 L. The L cancels and leaves 2500 mL. If you add it upside down, L ends up squared on the bottom and mL is on the bottom too, and nothing cancels.

    2. Turn a rate into another rate: 72 km/h to m/s needs two factors.

      What you should see

      One factor changes km to m (1000 m / 1 km). The other changes h to s (1 h / 3600 s), flipped so that h cancels. The answer is 20 m/s.

    3. Go from grams to a count of particles. Which two conversions does it take?

      What you should see

      Grams to moles uses the molar mass (1 mol / 58.44 g). Moles to particles uses Avogadro's number. The gram units cancel first, then the moles.

    Worked example

    How many hydrogen atoms are in 5.0 g of water?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Start with what you have and what you want. You have 5.0 g H₂O. You want atoms of H.
    2. Grams to moles of water. The molar mass of H₂O is 18.02 g/mol, so multiply by (1 mol H₂O / 18.02 g H₂O). The grams cancel.
    3. Moles of water to moles of H. Each H₂O has 2 H, so multiply by (2 mol H / 1 mol H₂O). The moles of water cancel.
    4. Moles of H to atoms. Multiply by (6.022 × 10²³ atoms H / 1 mol H). The moles of H cancel, leaving atoms of H.
    5. Calculate. 5.0 × (1/18.02) × 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.3 × 10²³ atoms of H. Two significant figures, because 5.0 has two.

    Common mistakes

    • Multiplying by the factor the wrong way up. If the units don't cancel, flip it.
    • Dropping units partway through, so there is nothing to check the setup against.
    • Using a factor with mismatched labels, such as mol NaCl in one place and mol Na in another. They are different things and don't cancel.
    • Rounding in the middle of a chain. Keep a few extra digits and round once at the end.
    • Forgetting that squared or cubed units need squared or cubed factors: 1 m³ is (100 cm)³ = 10⁶ cm³, not 100 cm³.

    How to set up any conversion

    Write the starting quantity with its unit. Choose a factor with the unit you want to remove in the denominator. Cancel. Repeat until only the target unit remains. The numerical value of each factor comes from a definition (1 kg = 1000 g), a measured property (molar mass, density) or a chemical formula (2 H per H₂O).

    Exact definitions such as 1000 mL = 1 L never limit significant figures. Measured values such as a molar mass can. Molar masses are usually given to enough digits not to be the weakest link.

    Check yourself: to convert 3.2 km to m, which factor do you multiply by?

    Scientific notation & sig figs

    A measurement is only as good as its last trusted digit. Significant figures say how many digits you can trust. Scientific notation writes very big and very small numbers without a pile of zeros.

    The idea

    If a ruler is marked in millimeters and you read 12.3 cm, the 3 is your best guess between marks. Every digit you are sure of, plus that one guessed digit, is significant. Writing 12.30 claims you could read to the next digit; writing 12 claims you could not.

    Zeros are only placeholders when they sit before the first nonzero digit (0.0045), or at the end of a number with no decimal point (1200). A trailing zero after a decimal point (1.200) is a claim of precision, so it counts.

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. How many significant figures are in 1200? What about 1200. and 1.200e3?

      What you should see

      1200 has two (the zeros are ambiguous placeholders), 1200. has four (the decimal point says the zeros were measured), and 1.200 × 10³ has four. Scientific notation removes the ambiguity.

    2. Multiply 4.56 by 1.4. How many digits can you keep?

      What you should see

      The calculator shows 6.384, but 1.4 has only two significant figures, so the answer is 6.4. For multiplying and dividing, count significant figures and keep the fewest.

    3. Add 12.11 and 0.023. Which number sets the limit?

      What you should see

      The sum is 12.133, but 12.11 is only known to the hundredths column, so the answer is 12.13. For adding and subtracting, line up the decimal points and keep the fewest decimal places.

    Worked example

    A student weighs 2.50 g of a salt, then measures its volume as 1.2 mL. Report the density with the right number of significant figures.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Count significant figures. 2.50 has three (the trailing zero is after a decimal point). 1.2 has two.
    2. Pick the rule. Density is mass ÷ volume: a division, so keep the fewest significant figures. That is two.
    3. Calculate. 2.50 / 1.2 = 2.0833… on the calculator.
    4. Round once, at the end. Two significant figures: 2.1 g/mL.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting leading zeros: 0.0045 has two significant figures, not four.
    • Dropping a trailing zero that matters: 2.50 is not the same claim as 2.5.
    • Using the multiply rule for addition, or the other way round. Sig figs for × ÷; decimal places for + −.
    • Rounding in the middle of a multi-step problem. Round once, at the end.
    • Counting exact numbers (like 12 eggs, or 1000 mL = 1 L) as if they limited the answer. Exact numbers have unlimited significant figures.

    The two rounding rules

    Multiplying and dividing: the answer has as many significant figures as the input with the fewest. Adding and subtracting: the answer has as many decimal places as the input with the fewest. These are different because adding combines uncertainties in absolute terms (so what matters is the last column), while multiplying combines them in relative terms (so what matters is the count of digits).

    Scientific notation writes a number as m × 10n with 1 ≤ m < 10. Every digit in m is significant, so 6.022 × 10²³ has four. Moving the decimal point left raises the exponent; moving it right lowers it.

    Check yourself: how many significant figures are in 0.00450?

    Logs & pH math

    The concentration of H⁺ in water solutions spans fourteen powers of ten. A log scale squeezes that range into numbers from 0 to 14: that is all pH is.

    The idea

    Imagine a staircase where every step is ten times higher than the last. The step number tells you how many tens you have multiplied by, and that is what a logarithm is: log 1000 = 3 because 1000 = 10³. Going up one step on a log scale means multiplying by 10, not adding 1.

    pH is the step number for hydrogen ions, with a minus sign so that more acid gives a smaller number: pH = −log[H⁺]. A solution with pH 3 has ten times as many H⁺ as one with pH 4, and a hundred times as many as pH 5.

      What you should see

      [H⁺] = 10⁻³ at pH 3 and 10⁻⁵ at pH 5. The ratio is 10² = 100. Two steps on the staircase, two factors of ten.

    2. Look at the linear chart at pH 7. Can you see the bar? Now look at the log chart.

      What you should see

      On a linear scale [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ M is far too small to see next to 1 M. The log chart makes every pH visible, which is the reason chemists use it.

    3. Does a pH of 3.0 have the same precision as a pH of 3? Enter 1.0e-3 for [H⁺].

      What you should see

      pH = 3.00: two significant figures in [H⁺] give two digits after the decimal point in the pH. The digits before the decimal point only locate the power of ten.

    Worked example

    What is the pH of 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M HCl? Then, a solution has pH 4.37. What is [H⁺]?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. HCl is a strong acid. It ionizes completely, so [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M.
    2. Take the log. log(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = log 3.2 + log 10⁻⁴ = 0.505 − 4 = −3.495.
    3. Flip the sign. pH = 3.495, which rounds to 3.49. Two significant figures in 3.2 give two decimal places.
    4. Now go backward. pH 4.37 means [H⁺] = 10−4.37. On the calculator, press 10x (or 2nd log) and enter −4.37.
    5. Answer. 10−4.37 = 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ M. Check: pH 4.37 is between 4 (10⁻⁴) and 5 (10⁻⁵), so [H⁺] should lie between them. It does.

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the minus sign: pH = −log[H⁺], and [H⁺] is a number less than 1, so its log is negative.
    • Thinking pH 3 is "a little more acidic" than pH 4. It is ten times more.
    • Reporting too many decimal places. The decimals in a pH equal the significant figures in the concentration.
    • Using ln instead of log. Chemistry's pH uses log base 10.
    • Going back with the wrong key: [H⁺] = 10−pH, not −10pH or log(pH).

    Why decimal places match significant figures

    The number before the decimal point in a log only says which power of ten you are at (the "characteristic"). Only the digits after the decimal point carry information about the precision of the measurement (the "mantissa"). So 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ M (2 significant figures) gives pH = 3.49 (2 decimal places).

    Water autoionizes: [H⁺][OH⁻] = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Taking −log of both sides gives pH + pOH = 14.00. In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M, so pH = 7.00: neutral.

    Check yourself: how does [H⁺] at pH 3 compare with [H⁺] at pH 5?

    ICE-table algebra

    Equilibrium problems end in an equation with x in it. Sometimes x is tiny and can be ignored. Sometimes it can't. This module shows you how to tell.

    The idea

    An ICE table tracks Initial, Change and Equilibrium amounts. For a weak acid HA with starting concentration c, the change is "−x for HA, +x for H⁺ and A⁻", so the equilibrium expression becomes K = x²/(c − x).

    If K is tiny compared with c, then x is tiny compared with c, and c − x is almost just c. Then x ≈ √(Kc). The trick is to check that the shortcut was fair: if x turns out to be more than 5% of c, go back and solve the quadratic.

    Try this

    Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

    1. A weak acid with K = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ at 0.10 M. Is the shortcut fair?

      What you should see

      x is about 0.13% of c, far below 5%. The two curves cross K almost at the same place and the shortcut error is tiny.

    2. Keep K the same, but dilute the acid to 0.0001 M. What happens to the shortcut?

      What you should see

      Now x is more than 5% of c (about 35%). The dashed curve separates from the solid one at K and the shortcut is wrong by a large amount. Weaker solutions are more ionized, so the shortcut fails first for dilute solutions.

    3. Go the other way: a large K at moderate concentration.

      What you should see

      With K = 0.01 and c = 0.10, x is about 27% of c. The shortcut fails whenever K is not much smaller than c; a handy test is c/K < 100 (roughly).

    Worked example

    A 0.050 M solution of a weak acid HA has Ka = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴. Find [H⁺].

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. ICE table. HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Initial: 0.050, 0, 0. Change: −x, +x, +x. Equilibrium: 0.050 − x, x, x.
    2. Write K. Ka = x² / (0.050 − x) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴.
    3. Try the shortcut. x ≈ √(4.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.050) = √(2.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.5 × 10⁻³.
    4. Check. 4.5 × 10⁻³ / 0.050 = 9%, which is more than 5%. The shortcut is not allowed.
    5. Solve the quadratic. x² + (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)x − 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 0, so x = [−4.0 × 10⁻⁴ + √((4.0 × 10⁻⁴)² + 4(2.0 × 10⁻⁵))] / 2 = 4.3 × 10⁻³ M. That is [H⁺].

    Common mistakes

    • Using the shortcut without checking it. State the check on paper every time: x/c × 100 < 5%.
    • Checking against the wrong number. Compare x with the starting concentration of the species that is being subtracted from.
    • Rearranging the quadratic wrongly: x² + Kx − Kc = 0 (the sign of the Kc term is negative).
    • Keeping the negative root of the quadratic. A concentration cannot be negative.
    • Forgetting that the 5% rule is a rule of thumb; if x is 6% your answer is off by about 3%, not ruined.

    Setting up and solving

    Rearrange K = x²/(c − x) into a standard quadratic: x² + Kx − Kc = 0. Then x = [−K + √(K² + 4Kc)] / 2. Take only the positive root.

    A faster way to decide: if c/K > 400 the shortcut error is under about 5%, and the x-is-small assumption holds. The slider above lets you watch the boundary.

    VSEPR shapes & polarity

    Electron clouds repel each other, so they spread out as far as they can in three dimensions. The shape of a molecule is the arrangement of the atoms sitting in that spread-out cloud.

    The idea

    Tie two balloons together by their knots and they point in opposite directions. Tie three and they make a flat triangle. Tie four and they form a pyramid with a triangular base, a tetrahedron. Nothing holds them in that shape except that each balloon pushes on the others.

    Around a central atom, every region of electron density is a balloon: a bond (single, double or triple, they all count as one region) or a lone pair. Negative charges repel, so the regions spread out. Then you name the shape using only the atoms, because the lone pairs are invisible in the final shape but they still take up room.

    3. Carbon dioxide has polar bonds. Is the molecule polar? Turn on the bond dipoles.

      What you should see

      Each C=O bond dipole points toward oxygen, but the two arrows point in opposite directions and cancel. CO₂ is nonpolar. Now show SO₂ and compare: bent, so the dipoles do not cancel.

    4. Is a symmetric molecule always nonpolar? Compare CCl₄ with CH₂Cl₂.

      What you should see

      Both are tetrahedral. In CCl₄ all four dipoles are identical and cancel. In CH₂Cl₂ the two C–Cl dipoles are bigger than the two C–H dipoles, so there is a leftover net dipole: polar.

    Worked example

    Predict the electron geometry, the molecular shape, the approximate bond angle and the polarity of SO₂.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Valence electrons. S has 6 and each O has 6: 6 + 2(6) = 18 electrons. Draw O–S–O with S in the middle and spread the rest as lone pairs to complete octets. Moving one lone pair from an O into a double bond with S gives every atom an octet; the structure has two resonance forms.
    2. Count regions of electron density around S. One double bond, one single bond and one lone pair: a double bond is a single region, so there are 3 regions.
    3. Electron geometry. Three regions spread out to a flat triangle: trigonal planar, with ideal angles of 120°.
    4. Molecular shape. Name the shape from the atoms only. Two O atoms and a lone pair: bent. The lone pair is a fatter balloon, so the O–S–O angle is a little less than 120°; it is about 119°.
    5. Polarity. Oxygen (3.44) is more electronegative than sulfur (2.58), so each S–O bond dipole points toward O. In a bent shape the two dipoles do not point in opposite directions, so they do not cancel: SO₂ is polar.

    Common mistakes

    • Counting only bonded atoms. Water has four regions around oxygen (two bonds, two lone pairs), not two.
    • Counting a double or triple bond as two or three regions. Any multiple bond is one region.
    • Naming the shape from the electron geometry. Water's electron geometry is tetrahedral; its shape is bent.
    • Thinking polar bonds always make a polar molecule. CO₂ and CCl₄ have polar bonds and are nonpolar, because the dipoles cancel.
    • Thinking a symmetric-looking shape guarantees nonpolar. The outer atoms must also be identical; CH₂Cl₂ is polar.
    • Forgetting that lone pairs squeeze bond angles: 109.5° → 107° (one lone pair) → 104.5° (two).

    From the picture to the table

    The steps are always the same: draw the Lewis structure, count the regions of electron density around the central atom, spread them out (electron geometry), then name the shape using only the atoms (molecular geometry). A lone pair is held by one nucleus instead of two, so it spreads out more and pushes the bonded pairs closer together. That is why the angles shrink: lone pair–lone pair repulsion is stronger than lone pair–bond repulsion, which is stronger than bond–bond repulsion.

    Polarity is a second question. Each bond between different atoms has a dipole: an arrow pointing toward the more electronegative atom. A molecule is polar only if the arrows add up to something that is not zero. Symmetric shapes with identical outer atoms cancel; bent and pyramidal shapes almost never do.
