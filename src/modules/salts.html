AP Unit 8

Salt solutions, polyprotic acids & species vs pH

"Neutral salt" doesn't mean a neutral solution. The ions of a salt can be acids or bases themselves, and the pH decides which form of an acid is actually in the beaker.

The idea

When a salt dissolves, you get its cation and its anion. Ask where each came from. An ion from a strong acid or base (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, Br⁻, ClO₄⁻) is a bystander: it does nothing to the pH. An ion from a weak acid or base is not. The conjugate base of a weak acid (CH₃COO⁻, F⁻, CO₃²⁻) takes protons from water and makes OH⁻, so the solution is basic. The conjugate acid of a weak base (NH₄⁺) gives protons to water and makes H₃O⁺, so it is acidic. Small, highly charged metal ions such as Al³⁺ and Fe³⁺ pull on the O–H bonds of their hydrating water and are acidic as well.

The strength of that effect comes from Ka × Kb = Kw. The weaker the parent acid, the stronger its conjugate base. And for an acid with more than one removable proton (a polyprotic acid such as H₃PO₄ or H₂CO₃) each proton comes off with its own Ka, always Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3, because it is harder to remove a proton from an ion that is already negative. Which form dominates depends on the pH compared with each pKa: below the pKa the protonated form wins; above it, the deprotonated form wins; at pH = pKa they are equal.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed a salt solution is neutral (we only used it to make precipitates and spectators). Now we know the ions themselves can be weak acids or bases, so a salt like sodium acetate has a pH above 7 and ammonium chloride below 7.

Try this

Predict, then check.

  1. Dissolve sodium acetate. Is the solution acidic or basic? Why?

    What you should see

    Basic (pH about 8.9 at 0.10 M). Na⁺ is a bystander, but CH₃COO⁻ is the conjugate base of a weak acid and pulls a proton off water: CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻.

  2. Dissolve ammonium chloride.

    What you should see

    Acidic (pH about 5.1). Cl⁻ is a bystander; NH₄⁺ is the conjugate acid of weak NH₃ and gives a proton to water: NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺.

  3. Phosphoric acid at pH 7: which form dominates?

    What you should see

    H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ are about equal at pH 7.2 (the second pKa). Around neutral pH the two middle forms dominate, which is why phosphate buffers work in cells.

Worked example

Reusable hand warmers hold a supersaturated solution of sodium acetate. Find the pH of 0.10 M sodium acetate. (Ka of acetic acid = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Which ion reacts? Na⁺ comes from a strong base (a bystander). CH₃COO⁻ is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it reacts with water: CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻.
  2. Find Kb. Kb = Kw/Ka = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.8×10⁻⁵ = 5.6×10⁻¹⁰.
  3. ICE table. Kb = x²/(0.10 − x) ≈ x²/0.10, so x = [OH⁻] = √(5.6×10⁻¹⁰ × 0.10) = 7.5×10⁻⁶ M (far under 5% of 0.10).
  4. pH. pOH = −log(7.5×10⁻⁶) = 5.13, so pH = 14.00 − 5.13 = 8.87. Basic, as predicted.

Common mistakes

  • Calling every salt solution neutral. Only a salt made from a strong acid and a strong base (NaCl, KNO₃) is neutral.
  • Using Ka of the parent acid directly for the anion. The anion is a base: use Kb = Kw/Ka.
  • Letting the cation of a strong base (Na⁺) or the anion of a strong acid (Cl⁻) hydrolyze. They don't.
  • For a polyprotic acid, treating the second proton as if it matters for pH. Usually Ka1 ≫ Ka2, so the pH comes from the first dissociation alone.
  • Mixing up the direction: below the pKa (more acidic solution) the protonated form dominates.

Cheat sheet

Strong acid + strong base parent: neutral (NaCl). Strong base + weak acid: basic (NaCH₃COO, NaF, Na₂CO₃, NaCN). Weak base + strong acid: acidic (NH₄Cl). Weak acid + weak base: compare Ka of the cation with Kb of the anion; NH₄CH₃COO is nearly neutral because Ka(NH₄⁺) ≈ Kb(CH₃COO⁻). Small, highly charged cations (Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺): acidic.

Species vs pH: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), so [A⁻]/[HA] = 10(pH − pKa). Two pH units below the pKa the acid is 99% protonated; two units above, 99% deprotonated. This is also how indicators work: the two forms HIn and In⁻ have different colors, and the color changes where pH ≈ pKa of the indicator.

An amphiprotic species can act as an acid or a base: water, HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, HSO₄⁻ (as an acid only toward water in practice), HPO₄²⁻. A solution of such an ion is often close to the average of the two neighboring pKa values.
