Light & Beer–Lambert

Light is a stream of energy packets called photons. Matter absorbs only photons with the right energy, which is how chemists identify substances and measure how much of them there is.

The idea

Light behaves as a wave with a wavelength and frequency, and as a stream of particles called photons. The two descriptions are tied together: a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency and a more energetic photon. A gamma-ray photon packs a punch; a radio photon is feeble. Each region of the electromagnetic spectrum pairs with something in a molecule: X-rays with inner electrons, UV and visible with valence electrons, infrared with vibrating bonds, microwaves with spinning molecules.

If a photon's energy exactly matches a gap in a molecule's energy levels, the molecule can absorb it. A colored solution absorbs the light that matches its gaps and lets the rest through. The more particles in the light's path, the more photons are absorbed: that is the Beer–Lambert law, and it lets you read concentration from how much light is lost.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Where is visible light on the spectrum, and how much energy does one mole of its photons carry?

    What you should see

    Visible light is a thin slice (400–700 nm) in the middle. A mole of 500 nm photons carries about 240 kJ, enough to excite the valence electrons of many molecules, and in the range of the energy of a chemical bond.

  2. Shine light just below the threshold frequency, then just above.

    What you should see

    Below the threshold no electrons come out, no matter how intense the light. Above it electrons are ejected immediately. Turning up the intensity then increases the number of electrons but not their energy: only a higher frequency does that.

  3. Double the concentration of a dye. What happens to the absorbance?

    What you should see

    Absorbance doubles (it is proportional to concentration), but the transmitted light does not halve: it falls exponentially. That is why the graph of A against c is a straight line, and why chemists plot A, not transmittance.

Worked example

A violet laser has wavelength 405 nm. Find its frequency, the energy of one photon, and the energy of one mole of photons.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Convert the wavelength. 405 nm = 4.05 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. Frequency. ν = c/λ = (2.998 × 10⁸ m/s) / (4.05 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 7.40 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Energy of one photon. E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(7.40 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹) = 4.90 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. Energy of a mole. (4.90 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)(6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹) = 2.95 × 10⁵ J/mol = 295 kJ/mol.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to convert nanometers to meters before using c = λν.
  • Thinking brighter light ejects faster electrons. Intensity controls how many; frequency controls how energetic.
  • Thinking longer wavelength means higher energy. Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.
  • Plotting transmittance against concentration and expecting a line. Absorbance is the one that is linear.
  • Choosing the wrong wavelength for a measurement. Use the wavelength where the sample absorbs most (the color complementary to what you see).

Why this matters in chemistry

A spectrophotometer measures absorbance at one wavelength. You prepare solutions of known concentration, measure their absorbance, and draw a calibration line (A against c); the slope is εb. Then you measure an unknown and read its concentration off the line. The law fails at high concentration, where particles interact or too little light gets through, so keep absorbances under about 1.

The photoelectric effect (no electrons below a threshold frequency) was what convinced physicists that light comes in packets: if light were just a wave, enough intensity should eventually free electrons at any frequency. The same idea gives photoelectron spectroscopy, where the measured electron energies reveal binding energies.
