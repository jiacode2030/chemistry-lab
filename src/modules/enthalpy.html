Enthalpy, Hess's law & bond energies

Chemical bonds are energy stores. Breaking them costs energy, making them gives energy back, and the difference is the heat of the reaction.

The idea

Think of a reaction as taking apart a Lego build and making a new one. Pulling apart the old bonds takes work (energy in); snapping together new bonds gives energy back (energy out). If more energy comes out than went in, the reaction is exothermic and ΔH is negative. If more went in than came out, it's endothermic and ΔH is positive.

Enthalpy (H) is a state function: ΔH depends only on where you start and finish, not the route. That gives Hess's law: if a reaction can be written as the sum of other reactions, its ΔH is the sum of theirs. You can flip a reaction (flip the sign) or multiply it (multiply ΔH), just like the coefficients. You can find the ΔH of a reaction you can't measure by adding ones you can.

Assumption upgrade Before, we measured heat directly with a calorimeter. Now we can calculate ΔH without doing the experiment, using Hess's law, standard enthalpies of formation, or average bond energies. Bond energies are averages over many molecules, so they give only an estimate.

    What you should see

Worked example

Charcoal burned with too little air makes poisonous carbon monoxide, which is why grills must never be used indoors. Find ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) using: (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH = −393.5 kJ; (2) CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH = −283.0 kJ.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Place each species. The target has C as a reactant, so keep equation (1) as written (C is on the left). The target has CO as a product, but in (2) CO is a reactant, so reverse (2).
  2. Reverse (2). CO₂ → CO + ½O₂, and flip the sign: ΔH = +283.0 kJ.
  3. Add. C + O₂ + CO₂ → CO₂ + CO + ½O₂. Cancel CO₂ on both sides and ½O₂ from the O₂: C + ½O₂ → CO. ✓
  4. Add the ΔH values. (−393.5) + (+283.0) = −110.5 kJ.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to change the sign when reversing a reaction, or forgetting to multiply ΔH when multiplying the equation.
  • Subtracting in the wrong order: it's products minus reactants for formation enthalpies, but bonds broken minus bonds formed for bond energies.
  • Forgetting to multiply each ΔH°f by its coefficient.
  • Using ΔH°f ≠ 0 for an element in its standard state (O₂, H₂, C(graphite), Fe(s)...). Those are all zero.
  • Confusing the state: H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) have different ΔH°f.

Per mole of what?

ΔH in a balanced equation is for the reaction as written. If you write 2CH₄ + 4O₂ → …, ΔH doubles. To find the heat for a specific amount, convert it to moles, divide by the coefficient, and multiply by ΔH. Bond energies are always positive numbers (breaking costs energy), but the energy of formation is released, so subtract it.
