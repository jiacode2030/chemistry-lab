Solubility equilibria & Ksp

"Insoluble" salts still dissolve a tiny bit. Ksp measures how much, and tells you when a precipitate will appear.

The idea

Drop a crystal of silver chloride in water. A few Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ ions break away; others stick back onto the crystal. Soon the rates match: a saturated solution, which is just an equilibrium, AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). Since the solid's concentration is constant, we leave it out: Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. The smaller the Ksp, the less dissolves.

For a salt MaXb, Ksp = [M]a[X]b. The molar solubility s is how many moles dissolve per liter: [M] = a·s and [X] = b·s. If you already have one of the ions in the solution (a common ion), Le Châtelier pushes the equilibrium left and the salt dissolves less. And if you mix solutions so that Q > Ksp, a precipitate forms until Q falls back to Ksp.

Assumption upgrade Before, "soluble" or "insoluble" from the solubility rules was a yes or no. Now we put numbers on it: insoluble salts have a small but nonzero solubility, and we can predict exactly when a precipitate forms.

Try this

Predict first, then use the labs.

  1. Add 0.10 M of the common chloride ion to AgCl. How does the solubility change?

    What you should see

    It drops by about four orders of magnitude, from 1.3×10⁻⁵ M to 1.8×10⁻⁹ M. Extra Cl⁻ pushes AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ to the left, so less dissolves.

  2. Compare Ag₂CrO₄ (Ksp 1.1×10⁻¹²) with AgCl (1.8×10⁻¹⁰). Which has the lower solubility?

    What you should see

    Surprise: Ag₂CrO₄ is more soluble (6.5×10⁻⁵ M vs 1.3×10⁻⁵ M) despite its smaller Ksp, because it has a different formula type. You can compare Ksp values directly only for salts with the same number of ions.

  3. Mix 0.001 M Ag⁺ and 0.001 M Cl⁻ in equal volumes. Precipitate?

    What you should see

    After mixing, each ion is 5×10⁻⁴ M, so Q = 2.5×10⁻⁷, which is much larger than Ksp (1.8×10⁻¹⁰): a precipitate forms.

Worked example

Most kidney stones are calcium oxalate, CaC₂O₄, with Ksp = 2.3 × 10⁻⁹. (a) Find its molar solubility in pure water. (b) Urine has about 2.5 × 10⁻³ M Ca²⁺. What is the most oxalate that can stay dissolved? (c) Typical urine has 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ M oxalate. Can a stone form?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Equilibrium and expression. CaC₂O₄(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + C₂O₄²⁻(aq), Ksp = [Ca²⁺][C₂O₄²⁻]. The solid is left out.
  2. (a) Use s. In pure water [Ca²⁺] = [C₂O₄²⁻] = s, so s² = 2.3 × 10⁻⁹ and s = 4.8 × 10⁻⁵ M. With M = 128.10 g/mol that is 6.1 × 10⁻³ g, about 6 mg, per liter.
  3. (b) Common ion. Now [Ca²⁺] is set by the urine: [C₂O₄²⁻]max = Ksp/[Ca²⁺] = 2.3 × 10⁻⁹ / 2.5 × 10⁻³ = 9.2 × 10⁻⁷ M, about 50 times less than in pure water.
  4. (c) Compare Q with Ksp. Q = (2.5 × 10⁻³)(3.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 7.5 × 10⁻⁷, more than 300 times Ksp. Q > Ksp, so the urine is supersaturated and crystals can form. (Natural inhibitors in urine usually stop them from growing.)
  5. Why doctors say drink water. Doubling the volume of urine halves both [Ca²⁺] and [C₂O₄²⁻], so Q drops by a factor of 4.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that [X] = b·s: for PbI₂ the iodide concentration is 2s, and it is squared in Ksp.
  • Comparing Ksp values to rank solubilities when the salts have different formulas (like AgCl and Ag₂CrO₄).
  • Forgetting dilution when two solutions are mixed: concentrations drop before you compute Q.
  • Including the solid in the Ksp expression.
  • Calling Q > Ksp "saturated". Q = Ksp is saturated; Q > Ksp is supersaturated and a precipitate forms.

Other ways to change solubility

Adding acid dissolves salts whose anion is a base (CaCO₃, Mg(OH)₂, many fluorides and sulfides): H⁺ removes the anion, pulling the equilibrium right. Complex-ion formation (like Ag⁺ with NH₃) works the same way. Temperature changes Ksp itself, as with any K.
