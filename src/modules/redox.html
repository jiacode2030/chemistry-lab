Oxidation states & redox

In a redox reaction, electrons move from one species to another. Oxidation numbers are the bookkeeping that tells you who gave and who took.

The idea

Drop a strip of zinc into blue copper(II) sulfate solution. The zinc dissolves and a red-brown crust of copper appears on it, and the blue fades. Zinc atoms are handing electrons to copper ions. The one that loses electrons is oxidized; the one that gains electrons is reduced. The mnemonic: OIL RIG, Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Oxidation and reduction always go together, because electrons can't vanish. The substance that gets oxidized is the reducing agent (it reduces the other one); the substance that gets reduced is the oxidizing agent. To follow electrons in molecules, where nothing is cleanly lost or gained, we assign oxidation numbers, the charge an atom would have if every bond were completely ionic.

Find the oxidation number

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Zinc in copper(II) solution. Which loses electrons?

    What you should see

    Zn goes from 0 to +2: oxidized (it is the reducing agent). Cu²⁺ goes from +2 to 0: reduced (the oxidizing agent). Each zinc atom releases 2 electrons that one Cu²⁺ ion picks up.

  2. Copper in silver nitrate: why two silver ions for each copper atom?

    What you should see

    Each Cu atom gives up 2 electrons, but each Ag⁺ can accept only 1. So two Ag⁺ are reduced for each Cu oxidized: electrons lost must equal electrons gained.

  3. Find the oxidation number of manganese in KMnO₄.

    What you should see

    K is +1 (group 1) and O is −2 (4 oxygens: −8). The compound is neutral, so +1 + x − 8 = 0 and Mn is +7. That unusually high number is why permanganate is such a strong oxidizing agent.

Worked example

Aluminum metal burns in chlorine gas: 2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃. (Aluminum chloride is the active ingredient in some antiperspirants.) Identify what is oxidized and reduced, the oxidizing and reducing agents, and the number of electrons transferred.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Assign oxidation numbers. Elements on their own are 0: Al = 0 and Cl₂ = 0. In AlCl₃, Cl is −1 (it is more electronegative), so Al is +3.
  2. See what changed. Al: 0 → +3 (increase: oxidized). Cl: 0 → −1 (decrease: reduced).
  3. Name the agents. The oxidized species, Al, is the reducing agent. The reduced species, Cl₂, is the oxidizing agent.
  4. Count the electrons. 2 Al atoms lose 3 e⁻ each: 6 e⁻. 3 Cl₂ is 6 Cl atoms, each gaining 1: 6 e⁻. They match. 6 electrons are transferred.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up which is which: the substance that is oxidized is the reducing agent, not the oxidizing agent.
  • Treating oxidation numbers as real charges in covalent compounds. In CO₂ carbon is +4 by convention, not a C⁴⁺ ion.
  • Forgetting the exceptions: H is −1 in metal hydrides (NaH), O is −1 in peroxides (H₂O₂).
  • Forgetting that the oxidation numbers in a polyatomic ion sum to its charge, not to zero.
  • Thinking only reactions with oxygen are oxidations. Oxidation is loss of electrons, with or without oxygen.

Rules for assigning oxidation numbers

(1) An atom in a pure element is 0. (2) A monatomic ion has its charge. (3) The numbers in a neutral compound add to 0; in an ion they add to its charge. (4) Group 1 metals are +1, group 2 metals +2. (5) Fluorine is −1. (6) Hydrogen is +1 (−1 with metals); oxygen is −2 (−1 in peroxides). (7) Other halogens are −1 unless combined with oxygen or fluorine. Use the rules in this order of priority; the one unknown atom gets whatever makes the sum right.

A reaction is a redox reaction if any oxidation number changes. Combustion, corrosion, batteries, respiration and bleaching are all redox processes.
