Intermolecular forces

Molecules attract each other without bonding. How strongly decides whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature, and how hot it must get to boil.

The idea

Inside a molecule, covalent bonds hold atoms together. Between molecules, much weaker intermolecular forces (IMFs) act like faint Velcro. To boil a liquid you do not break any bonds in the molecules: you only pull the molecules away from each other. So the stronger the Velcro, the more heat is needed, and the higher the boiling point.

There are three kinds, all from Coulomb's law. London dispersion forces exist in every substance: electrons slosh around and make momentary dipoles; bigger electron clouds slosh more, so larger molecules attract more. Dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding is an especially strong dipole–dipole force when H is bonded to N, O or F and points at a lone pair on another N, O or F.

Boiling points of the hydrides

Down each group the molar mass and electron cloud grow, so dispersion forces grow and the boiling point rises. NH₃, H₂O and HF break the trend: hydrogen bonding makes them boil far above the rest of their group.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Compare argon and water at 200 K. Which clumps?

    What you should see

    At 200 K argon (boils at 87 K) is a gas of separate atoms, while water (boils at 373 K) is held together as a solid or liquid clump. Same temperature, very different attractions.

  2. Warm water slowly from 100 K up to 500 K.

    What you should see

    The cluster loosens and molecules start to escape around 373 K. The molecules themselves are unchanged: only the distances between them grow. Boiling overcomes IMFs, not covalent bonds.

  3. Compare methane with butane. Both are nonpolar: why does butane boil higher?

    What you should see

    Butane has many more electrons, so a bigger, more polarizable cloud and stronger dispersion forces. It is still a clump at 150 K when methane (boiling at 112 K) has flown apart.

Worked example

Rank CH₄, CH₃OH and CH₃Cl from lowest to highest boiling point, and name the strongest IMF in each.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. CH₄. Nonpolar, so only London dispersion forces, and a small molecule: the weakest attraction.
  2. CH₃Cl. Polar (the C–Cl bond is polar and the shape is not symmetric), so dipole–dipole forces plus dispersion. Stronger than CH₄.
  3. CH₃OH. Has an O–H bond, so it can hydrogen bond with other molecules: the strongest IMF of the three.
  4. Ranking. CH₄ (112 K) < CH₃Cl (249 K) < CH₃OH (338 K), the order of increasing IMF strength.

Common mistakes

  • Saying boiling breaks the bonds inside the molecule. Boiling water gives H₂O vapor, not hydrogen and oxygen.
  • Calling any molecule with H a hydrogen-bonder. The H must be bonded to N, O or F (CH₄ and HCl do not).
  • Forgetting that dispersion forces exist in every substance, and dominate in large nonpolar molecules.
  • Mixing up intermolecular forces with covalent bond strength: the covalent bonds in water are far stronger than the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.
  • Comparing boiling points without considering size: a large nonpolar molecule can beat a small polar one.

Ranking by strength

For molecules of similar size, the order is: hydrogen bonding > dipole–dipole > London dispersion. For molecules of very different sizes, dispersion forces can win, so compare molar masses too. Ionic substances (full charges) are held by even stronger forces, and in a solution an ion is attracted to the partial charges on water (ion–dipole).

These attractions also explain surface tension, viscosity, vapor pressure and solubility. Stronger IMFs mean higher boiling point, higher viscosity, higher surface tension and lower vapor pressure at a given temperature.
