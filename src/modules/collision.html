Collision theory, activation energy & catalysts

Why does heating speed a reaction up, and how can a catalyst do it without heat? The answer is a hill and a crowd of particles.

The idea

For particles to react they must collide, hard enough and facing the right way. Think of pushing a ball over a hill: only a ball with enough energy gets to the other side. The height of that hill is the activation energy, Ea. Most collisions are too gentle and the particles just bounce apart.

At any temperature, particles have a spread of energies (the Boltzmann distribution). Raising the temperature does two things, but the big one is that it stretches the high-energy tail, so many more collisions clear the hill. A catalyst doesn't heat anything: it offers a different path with a lower hill, so a much larger share of ordinary collisions succeed. It is not used up and doesn't change the overall ΔE.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed that every collision between reactants makes products, so we only needed to count collisions. Now we also need enough energy and the right orientation, so only a small fraction of collisions are effective.

Try this

Predict, then look at both the box and the graph.

  1. Heat the gas by 100 K. By roughly what factor does the number of high-energy collisions change?

    What you should see

    The tail above Eₐ grows by a factor of tens to thousands for a typical Eₐ, while the average energy rises only a third. That is why a 10 K rise can double a rate.

  2. Add a catalyst at 300 K. Compare with heating.

    What you should see

    The temperature (and the curve) hasn't changed, but the Eₐ marker moved left into a much fatter part of the distribution. The fraction of effective collisions rose by many orders of magnitude.

  3. On the energy hill, add the catalyst. Does ΔH change?

    What you should see

    The barrier shrinks for both directions, but reactants and products stay at the same heights. ΔH is the same; only the speed changes.

Worked example

A reaction has Ea = 75 kJ/mol. By what factor does its rate constant increase when T rises from 300 K to 310 K? (R = 8.314 J/mol·K)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Use the Arrhenius form for two temperatures. ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂).
  2. Convert units. Ea = 75 000 J/mol so it matches R in J.
  3. Compute. 1/300 − 1/310 = 1.075×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Ea/R = 75000/8.314 = 9.02×10³ K. The product is 0.970.
  4. Exponentiate. k₂/k₁ = e0.970 = 2.6. A 10 K rise multiplies the rate by about 2.6, the old "doubles every 10 °C" rule of thumb (it depends on Eₐ).

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a catalyst supplies energy or changes ΔH. It lowers Eₐ for both the forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Saying heat makes every particle go faster by the same amount. Temperature widens the whole distribution; the tail grows most.
  • Forgetting to convert Eₐ from kJ to J, or to use Kelvin, in the Arrhenius equation.
  • Saying a catalyst is a reactant. It is consumed in one step and regenerated in another, so it doesn't appear in the net equation.
  • Treating "more collisions" as the whole story. Most of the speed-up from heating comes from more energetic collisions, not more frequent ones.

The Arrhenius equation

The rate constant is k = A·e−Ea/RT. The factor A bundles collision frequency and orientation. The exponential factor is roughly the fraction of collisions with energy of at least Ea. A plot of ln k against 1/T is a straight line with slope −Ea/R.

Types of catalysts

A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (aqueous I⁻ catalyzing H₂O₂ decomposition; Cl radicals in the atmosphere). A heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase, usually a solid surface: reactant molecules adsorb onto the metal, their bonds weaken, they react, and the products leave (a catalytic converter's Pt/Pd/Rh; iron in the Haber process). Enzymes are biological catalysts: a substrate binds to the enzyme's active site, which holds it in the right orientation and stabilizes the transition state. All catalysts lower the activation energy by providing a different mechanism; none changes ΔH or the equilibrium position.
