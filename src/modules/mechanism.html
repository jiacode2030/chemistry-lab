Reaction mechanisms

Most reactions don't happen in one step. A mechanism is the movie of what really happens, and the slowest scene sets the speed.

The idea

Imagine a busy sandwich shop: one person slices bread (fast), one adds fillings (fast), and one wraps each sandwich, slowly. However fast the first two work, orders go out at the speed of the wrapper. In a reaction, that slow stage is the rate-determining step, and it's the step with the highest energy hill.

A mechanism is a list of elementary steps: single collisions or breakups. For an elementary step, and only for those, the rate law comes straight from the coefficients: A + B → C has rate = k[A][B]. Species made in one step and used up in a later one are intermediates: they never appear in the overall equation, and they can't appear in the final rate law. A catalyst is the reverse: used first, then regenerated.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed a balanced equation was a single event, so its coefficients could set the rate. Now we know the equation is only the sum of several steps, so the rate law must come from the slow step.

    Try this

    Predict first, then use the labs above.

    1. Pick the NO₂ + CO reaction. Which species is in the net equation's reactants but is not in the rate law?

      What you should see

      CO. The rate law is rate = k[NO₂]²: CO takes part only in the fast second step, after the slow one, so changing [CO] doesn't change the speed.

    2. Pick H₂O₂ + I⁻. Why does iodide appear in the rate law but not in the overall equation?

      What you should see

      I⁻ is a catalyst: it reacts in the slow step (so it affects the rate) and is regenerated in the fast step (so it cancels in the net equation).

    3. Pick 2NO + O₂ and step through the rate law. What do we do with N₂O₂?

      What you should see

      The slow step contains an intermediate, so we replace [N₂O₂] using the fast equilibrium before it: forward rate = reverse rate gives [N₂O₂] ∝ [NO]².

    Worked example

    Proposed mechanism: Step 1 (slow): A + B → C + D. Step 2 (fast): C + B → E. Find the overall reaction, the intermediate and the rate law.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Add the steps. A + B + C + B → C + D + E. Cancel what appears on both sides (C): A + 2B → D + E.
    2. Find the intermediate. C is produced in step 1 and consumed in step 2, and isn't in the overall reaction. C is the intermediate.
    3. Find the slow step. Step 1 is slow, so it sets the rate.
    4. Write its rate law. Step 1 is elementary, so rate = k[A][B]. It has no intermediate on the left, so this is the answer. Note it is not k[A][B]², even though the overall reaction uses 2B.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the overall equation's coefficients for the rate law. Only an elementary step works that way.
    • Leaving an intermediate in the rate law. Express it with the earlier fast steps instead.
    • Confusing intermediates and catalysts: an intermediate is made then used up; a catalyst is used then made again.
    • Thinking a mechanism can be proved. Experiments can only show whether a mechanism agrees with the measured rate law.
    • Forgetting that the steps must add up to the overall reaction.

    Checking a mechanism

    A proposed mechanism must (1) add up to the overall equation, and (2) predict the experimental rate law. If the slow step is the first step, the rate law is read directly from it. If the slow step comes after a fast equilibrium, substitute for the intermediates using that equilibrium. Molecularity (unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular) is the number of particles in an elementary step; it is almost always 1 or 2.
