Integrated rate laws & half-life

Rate laws tell you how fast. Integrated rate laws tell you how much is left after a given time, and which graph turns into a straight line.

The idea

Watch a jar of reactant disappear. A zero-order reaction loses the same amount every second, like a tap emptying a bucket at a steady rate. A first-order reaction loses the same fraction every second, like a bank balance shrinking by 10% a day: fast at first, then slower. A second-order reaction slows down even more as it goes, because the reactants meet less and less often.

Each order has one graph that comes out as a straight line: [A] vs t (zero), ln[A] vs t (first), 1/[A] vs t (second). If you measure concentrations over time, the straight one tells you the order. The half-life is the time for [A] to fall to half. For first order it never changes, however much you start with. That is the signature of exponential decay.

Assumption upgrade Before, the rate law gave the rate at one instant, so we only needed concentrations at that instant. Now we want the concentration at a later time, so we need the integrated forms, and time enters the equations.

Mystery data: which order?

Which of the three graphs is a straight line? Its order is the answer.

Try this

Predict first, then check on the graphs.

  1. First order: play to 100 s and watch the jar. Is the second half-life the same length as the first?

    What you should see

    Yes: each half-life takes the same time. At t½ half is left, at 2t½ a quarter, at 3t½ an eighth. Only first order behaves like this.

  2. Zero order: does it ever run out?

    What you should see

    Yes. It loses a fixed amount per second, so it hits zero at a definite time, [A]₀/k. First and second order only approach zero.

  3. Second order: look at the 1/[A] graph.

    What you should see

    The 1/[A] graph is a straight line rising with slope k. The other two curve.

Worked example

The decomposition of N₂O₅ is first order with k = 5.0×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. Starting with 0.800 M, how much is left after 20.0 minutes, and what is the half-life?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Pick the equation. First order, and we want a concentration at a time: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt.
  2. Convert time to seconds so it matches k: 20.0 min × 60 = 1200 s.
  3. Compute kt. kt = (5.0×10⁻⁴)(1200) = 0.60. Then [A] = [A]₀·e−kt = 0.800 × e−0.60 = 0.800 × 0.549 = 0.439 M.
  4. Half-life. t½ = 0.693 / k = 0.693 / 5.0×10⁻⁴ = 1.4×10³ s (about 23 min). Check: 1200 s is a bit less than one half-life, so a bit more than half should be left. 0.439/0.800 = 55%. ✓

Common mistakes

  • Using minutes with a k in s⁻¹. Convert time first.
  • Using the first-order half-life formula for a second-order reaction. Only first order has a constant half-life, 0.693/k.
  • Forgetting the sign: the slope of ln[A] vs t is −k, so k itself is positive.
  • Using log base 10 where the equation needs ln.
  • Thinking a half-life means the reaction is "half done" at a fixed time regardless of order. In second order, each successive half-life is longer; in zero order, shorter.

Why first order is a straight ln plot

If the rate is proportional to [A], the amount lost each second is a fixed fraction of what is there, and the solution is exponential: [A] = [A]₀e−kt. Taking ln of both sides gives ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt, a straight line with slope −k. Second order and zero order integrate the same way and give their own lines.
