Orbital shapes in 3D

An orbital is not a track the electron follows. It is a map of where the electron is likely to be found. Take enough snapshots and the map appears.

The idea

Imagine photographing a bee near a hive a thousand times and drawing a dot for each photo. You never see its path, but the cloud of dots shows where it spends its time. The electron works like that, except that nobody can say where it is at any moment; quantum mechanics only gives the odds. Each orbital is a different pattern of odds.

The pattern has a size and a shape. An s orbital is a ball. A p orbital has two lobes, like a dumbbell, pointing along x, y or z. A d orbital has four lobes (or a donut with two). Higher shells (n) are bigger, and can have nodes: places where the electron is never found.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Watch a 1s cloud build. Where do the dots pile up?

    What you should see

    The cloud is densest near the center, but the graph below peaks at 1 a₀ (0.53 Å), not at the nucleus: the volume of a thin shell grows as r², and that beats the falling density. 90% of the time the electron is within about 2.7 a₀.

  2. Compare 1s, 2s and 3s. How do the sizes and node counts change?

    What you should see

    Each step up a shell makes the orbital larger and adds one radial node: a spherical surface where the electron is never found (the gap in the cloud and the dashed lines on the graph). The count is n − ℓ − 1.

  3. Show a whole p subshell: three dumbbells, one on each axis.

    What you should see

    The three p orbitals point along x, y and z, at right angles to each other. They have the same energy in an atom. Each has one angular node: the plane through the nucleus perpendicular to its lobes.

  4. Compare the five d orbitals.

    What you should see

    Four of the d orbitals are four-lobed clovers (they differ only in orientation); dz² is two lobes with a donut around the middle. d orbitals have two angular nodes.

Worked example

How many radial and angular nodes does a 3p orbital have, and how many orbitals are in the n = 3 shell?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Identify n and ℓ. 3p means n = 3 and ℓ = 1 (s = 0, p = 1, d = 2).
  2. Angular nodes. The number of angular nodes equals ℓ, so a 3p orbital has 1.
  3. Radial nodes. n − ℓ − 1 = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1.
  4. Count the orbitals in the shell. n = 3 has subshells 3s (1 orbital), 3p (3) and 3d (5): 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 orbitals, which is n². With two electrons per orbital, the shell holds 18.

Common mistakes

  • Picturing the electron as a planet circling the nucleus on a flat track. An orbital is a 3D probability map.
  • Thinking the outline of an orbital is a wall. It is just the surface inside which the electron spends most (say 90%) of its time.
  • Thinking the p orbital's two lobes are two electrons. One electron (or a pair) is spread over the whole dumbbell.
  • Confusing the plus and minus signs of the lobes with electric charge. They are the sign of the wavefunction, which matters for bonding but isn't a charge.
  • Mixing up radial and angular nodes: radial nodes are spheres (n − ℓ − 1); angular nodes are planes or cones through the nucleus (ℓ).

Quantum numbers in one place

Three quantum numbers name an orbital. n (1, 2, 3 …) sets size and energy. ℓ (0 to n − 1) sets the shape: s, p, d, f. mℓ (from −ℓ to +ℓ) sets the orientation, so a subshell has 2ℓ + 1 orbitals. A fourth number, the spin ms (±½), tells which of the two electrons in an orbital you mean.

Nodes tell you the shape without the picture: total nodes = n − 1; angular nodes = ℓ; radial nodes = n − ℓ − 1.
