Periodic trends & Coulomb's law

Atomic size, ionization energy and electronegativity all come from one question: how hard does the nucleus pull on the outermost electron, and how far away is that electron?

The idea

Opposite charges attract, and the pull falls off fast with distance: Coulomb's law says the force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the distance squared. Double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter; double one charge and it doubles.

An outer electron feels the nucleus's positive charge, but the inner (core) electrons cancel part of it. That leaves an effective nuclear charge, roughly the number of protons minus the number of core electrons. Moving across a period, protons are added but the electrons go into the same shell, so the pull grows and the atom shrinks. Moving down a group, a whole new shell is added, so the outer electron is much farther away and the atom grows.

    What you should see

    The force falls to one quarter: double the distance, ¼ the force (inverse square).

  2. Go across period 2 from lithium to fluorine on the radius graph.

    What you should see

    The atom shrinks from 128 pm to 57 pm. Zeff climbs from 1 to 7 while the outer electron stays in shell 2, so the nucleus pulls harder on electrons at about the same distance.

  3. Go down group 1: lithium, sodium, potassium.

    What you should see

    Zeff stays at 1 but the atom grows because each step adds a shell. The outer electron is farther from the nucleus, so it is easier to remove: check the ionization-energy graph.

Worked example

Sodium reacts violently with water by losing an electron, while neon reacts with nothing. Explain why the first ionization energy of sodium (496 kJ/mol) is so much lower than that of neon (2081 kJ/mol).

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Compare the outer electrons. Neon's outer electron is in shell 2; sodium's is in shell 3 (3s¹).
  2. Compare the pull. Neon: Z = 10, core = 2, Zeff ≈ 8. Sodium: Z = 11, core = 10, Zeff ≈ 1.
  3. Compare the distance. Sodium's outer electron is in a larger shell, so it is farther from the nucleus than neon's.
  4. Use Coulomb's law. Neon's outer electron is closer to a much stronger effective charge, so the attraction is much greater and more energy is needed to remove it. Sodium's is held weakly, so its ionization energy is low.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "more protons means a bigger pull" without noticing the core electrons cancel most of it when you move down a group.
  • Answering with only "it is farther away". A full answer names both the charge felt (Zeff) and the distance (shell).
  • Thinking atoms get bigger across a period because they have more electrons. The electrons go into the same shell and the stronger pull wins.
  • Forgetting the small dips: first ionization energy drops from Be to B and from N to O, because of subshell and electron-pairing effects.
  • Mixing up atomic radius (shrinks across) with ionic radius (cations are smaller than their atoms; anions are larger).

Summary of the trends

Across a period (left to right): Zeff goes up, atomic radius goes down, first ionization energy generally goes up, electronegativity goes up. Down a group: the outer electron is in a larger shell, so radius goes up, ionization energy goes down, and electronegativity goes down.

Cations are smaller than their parent atoms (fewer electrons, and often a whole shell is gone, so the same nuclear pull is shared by fewer electrons). Anions are larger (more electrons repel each other while the nuclear pull is unchanged).

Electron affinity and isoelectronic ions

Electron affinity is the energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron. The halogens have the most negative (most favorable) values because adding one electron completes a stable octet and the nucleus pulls hard on it; the noble gases and group 2 elements are close to zero or unfavorable because the new electron would have to go into a higher, less stable subshell. Across a period electron affinity generally becomes more negative, with exceptions (N is less favorable than C, because the added electron must pair in a 2p orbital; Cl is slightly more favorable than F because F is so small that electron repulsion offsets the gain).

Isoelectronic species have the same number of electrons, such as N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ (all 10). Their sizes follow the nuclear charge alone: with the same electrons, the more protons, the stronger the pull and the smaller the particle. So N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺.
