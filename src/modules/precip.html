Net ionic equations & precipitation

Mix two clear solutions and sometimes a solid appears. The solid forms because two particular ions find each other and lock together; every other ion just watches.

The idea

Ionic compounds that dissolve in water exist as separate, moving ions. When two such solutions are mixed, every positive ion meets every negative ion. Most of these partners prefer to stay dissolved (they are soluble). But if one cation–anion pair forms an insoluble compound, the pair clumps into a solid that falls out of the solution: a precipitate.

The ions that never take part, floating along unchanged, are spectator ions. A net ionic equation deletes the spectators and shows only what actually happens. To predict whether a precipitate forms, swap the partners and check the solubility rules for each new compound.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Mix silver nitrate with sodium chloride. Which ions clump?

    What you should see

    Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ stick together as white AgCl and sink. Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ stay dissolved: they are the spectators. Net ionic equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

  2. Now mix sodium chloride with potassium nitrate.

    What you should see

    Nothing happens: every possible pair (NaNO₃, KCl) is soluble, so all four ions are spectators and there is no net ionic equation. Mixing is not the same as reacting.

  3. Mix lead(II) nitrate with potassium iodide. Why is the net equation different in form?

    What you should see

    The yellow solid PbI₂ needs two iodide ions per lead ion: Pb²⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂(s). The charges of the ions set the formula, and the subscript becomes the coefficient in the net ionic equation.

Worked example

Write the net ionic equation for mixing aqueous BaCl₂ and Na₂SO₄.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Dissociate. BaCl₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻. Na₂SO₄ → 2Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻.
  2. Swap partners. Possible products: BaSO₄ and NaCl.
  3. Check solubility. NaCl is soluble (all Na⁺ salts are). BaSO₄ is insoluble (sulfates are soluble except with Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Pb²⁺).
  4. Complete ionic equation. Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ + 2Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s) + 2Na⁺ + 2Cl⁻.
  5. Cancel spectators (Na⁺ and Cl⁻): Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). Check: charge 0 on both sides. ✓

Common mistakes

  • Writing a precipitate as separate ions. A solid is written as the whole formula, with (s).
  • Splitting a solid, a gas or a weak electrolyte into ions in the complete ionic equation. Only soluble ionic compounds (aq) are split.
  • Forgetting to check both new compounds. Only one needs to be insoluble for a reaction, but you must check both.
  • Forgetting that the net ionic equation must balance in charge as well as in atoms.
  • Saying a spectator ion "does nothing". It still carries charge and keeps the solution neutral, but it is not changed.

Three kinds of equation

The molecular equation writes every compound as a whole formula: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). The complete ionic equation splits every soluble ionic compound into its ions and leaves solids whole. The net ionic equation removes the spectators. All three are balanced, but the net ionic equation tells the real chemical story, and it is the one used for stoichiometry: the amount of precipitate depends only on the amount of the limiting ion.

Precipitation is used to remove ions from water (for example, hydroxide or sulfide precipitates remove heavy metals) and to detect them (a white precipitate with silver nitrate indicates chloride).
