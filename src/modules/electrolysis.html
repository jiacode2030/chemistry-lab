Electrolysis & Faraday's law

Run a battery backwards: push electrons through a cell with an outside power supply and force a reaction that wouldn't happen by itself.

The idea

A galvanic cell is a ball rolling downhill and giving you work. An electrolytic cell is the opposite: you use an outside power supply to push the ball uphill. The reaction has a negative E° (not spontaneous), so you must apply more voltage than |E°| to make it go. This is how aluminum, chlorine and sodium hydroxide are made, how metals are refined and plated, and how a rechargeable battery recharges.

The definitions don't change: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode (An Ox, Red Cat). What flips is the sign: in an electrolytic cell the cathode is wired to the negative terminal of the supply (it is where electrons are pushed in) and the anode to the positive terminal. Faraday's law counts the product: every mole of electrons (96 485 C) deposits 1/z mole of a metal ion of charge z⁺. The amount = (current × time) ÷ (F × z).

Assumption upgrade Before, we only used cells that run on their own. Now we force reactions with electricity, so in aqueous solutions water competes: it can be reduced at the cathode (making H₂) or oxidized at the anode (making O₂). Whichever reaction needs the least voltage happens first.

Try this

Predict the products first.

  1. Double the current. Does it plate twice as much in the same time?

    What you should see

    Yes: charge = I × t, so twice the current moves twice the electrons and deposits twice the mass. About 3.6 g of copper after 30 min at 6.00 A.

  2. Plate silver and copper with the same current and time. Which gives more mass?

    What you should see

    Silver: each Ag⁺ needs only one electron (z = 1) and an Ag atom is heavier (107.9 g/mol), so the same charge (5400 C at 3.00 A for 30 min) deposits 6.0 g of silver but only 1.8 g of copper (z = 2, 63.55 g/mol): about 3.4 times as much.

  3. Electrolyze aqueous sodium sulfate. Which ions react?

    What you should see

    Neither Na⁺ nor SO₄²⁻: water is reduced at the cathode (H₂) and oxidized at the anode (O₂). Na⁺ is too hard to reduce in water and sulfate is already fully oxidized. The net result is water splitting.

Worked example

A current of 2.50 A flows for 45.0 minutes through a solution of Cu²⁺. What mass of copper plates onto the cathode? (M = 63.55 g/mol, F = 96 485 C/mol)

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Charge. t = 45.0 min × 60 = 2700 s. q = I t = (2.50 A)(2700 s) = 6750 C.
  2. Moles of electrons. n(e⁻) = 6750 / 96485 = 0.06996 mol.
  3. Moles of copper. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so n(Cu) = 0.06996 / 2 = 0.03498 mol.
  4. Mass. 0.03498 mol × 63.55 g/mol = 2.22 g of copper.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to convert minutes or hours to seconds before multiplying by amperes.
  • Forgetting z: Cu²⁺ needs 2 electrons per atom, Al³⁺ needs 3.
  • Giving the cathode a "+" sign in an electrolytic cell. It is negative here (but still the site of reduction).
  • Predicting that Na or K metal forms from an aqueous solution. Water is reduced first, giving H₂.
  • Forgetting that applied voltage must exceed |E°cell| (and real cells need extra for overpotential).

Galvanic vs electrolytic

Galvanic: spontaneous (E° > 0), produces electricity, anode is −, cathode is +. Electrolytic: non-spontaneous (E° < 0), consumes electricity, anode is +, cathode is −. In both, oxidation is at the anode, reduction at the cathode, and electrons flow anode → cathode through the wire (the power supply pushes them in the electrolytic case).
