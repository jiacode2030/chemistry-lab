Acids, bases, pH & strong acids

Acid-base chemistry is proton hopping. Water itself takes part, and pH is just a convenient way to count the hydronium ions.

The idea

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor. Every acid-base reaction is a proton hand-off, and each side has a conjugate partner: the acid becomes its conjugate base, the base becomes its conjugate acid. In water, a free H⁺ doesn't exist alone: it sticks to a water molecule, making hydronium, H₃O⁺.

Even pure water does this a tiny bit: two H₂O molecules swap a proton (autoionization), giving Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. pH = −log[H₃O⁺], and pOH = −log[OH⁻], with pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C. A strong acid (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄ for its first proton) gives up its proton completely, so [H₃O⁺] equals the acid's concentration. Strong bases (hydroxides of group 1, and Ca, Sr, Ba) dissociate completely too.

Assumption upgrade Before, pH = −log(c) for a strong acid. Now we notice water supplies 10⁻⁷ M of H₃O⁺ too. At normal concentrations that doesn't matter, but for a very dilute acid (10⁻⁸ M) it does: the pH of 10⁻⁸ M HCl is about 6.98, not 8, because an acid can't make the solution basic.

Try this

Predict, then slide the controls.

  1. Dilute a strong acid by a factor of 10. How much does the pH change?

    What you should see

    Going from 10⁻² M to 10⁻³ M raises the pH by exactly 1 (from 2 to 3): each factor of 10 in concentration is one pH unit. Check by sliding.

  2. Dilute HCl all the way to 10⁻⁸ M. Does the pH go above 7?

    What you should see

    No: about 6.98. Water contributes 10⁻⁷ M of H₃O⁺ and the acid adds to that; an acid can never make the pH basic.

  3. Hot water: set the temperature to 50 °C and look at a neutral solution.

    What you should see

    Neutral pH is about 6.63 at 50 °C, not 7, because Kw grows with temperature. Neutral means [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻], not "pH = 7".

Worked example

What is the pH of 0.025 M Ba(OH)₂ at 25 °C?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Strong base: dissociates completely. Ba(OH)₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2OH⁻.
  2. Count hydroxide. Each formula unit gives 2 OH⁻, so [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.025 = 0.050 M.
  3. pOH. pOH = −log(0.050) = 1.30.
  4. pH. pH = 14.00 − 1.30 = 12.70.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to count the number of OH⁻ per formula unit (Ba(OH)₂ and Ca(OH)₂ give 2).
  • Giving a pH above 7 for a very dilute acid (or below 7 for a very dilute base).
  • Using pH + pOH = 14 at temperatures other than 25 °C: it equals −log Kw.
  • Saying H⁺ "floats free" in water. It's H₃O⁺, but H⁺ and H₃O⁺ are interchangeable on the AP exam.
  • Mixing up acid and conjugate: an acid's conjugate is a base (it has one fewer H⁺).

Strong acids and bases to know

Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, and H₂SO₄ (first proton only). Strong bases: LiOH, NaOH, KOH, RbOH, CsOH, Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂. For a strong acid, [H₃O⁺] = c; for a strong base, [OH⁻] = (number of OH) × c. Everything else is weak. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one H⁺: strong acids have negligible conjugate bases; weak acids have weak conjugate bases.
