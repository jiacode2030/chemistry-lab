Weak acids & bases, Ka and Kb

Most acids don't give up all their protons. A weak acid is a small chemical tug-of-war, and Ka says who is winning.

The idea

A strong acid is like a person who throws every ball they're holding. A weak acid holds on tight: only a few molecules per thousand hand over their proton. Acetic acid in vinegar is the classic example: in 0.10 M vinegar only about 1.3% of the molecules ionize, so almost all the acid is still sitting there as CH₃COOH.

The equilibrium HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻ has Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. A bigger Ka means a stronger acid. Weak bases work the same way with Kb: B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. An acid and its conjugate base are linked by Ka × Kb = Kw: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. Interestingly, the fraction that ionizes goes up when you dilute: more room for the ions to spread out.

Assumption upgrade Before, we assumed acids were strong, so [H₃O⁺] equalled the acid concentration. Now an acid can be weak, and [H₃O⁺] must come from an equilibrium (Ka) and an ICE table, with the "x is small" check.

Try this

Predict first, then compare the two beakers.

  1. Look at 0.10 M acetic acid. How many molecules ionize?

    What you should see

    About 1 in 75 (1.3%). The pH is about 2.88, far higher than the pH 1.00 of 0.10 M HCl, because most acetic acid stays whole.

  2. Dilute acetic acid tenfold, then tenfold again. What happens to the percent ionized?

    What you should see

    It rises (about 4% at 0.001 M): dilution favors the side with more particles, so a larger fraction of the molecules ionize. (The pH rises less than a strong acid's would.)

  3. Compare HF with HCN at the same concentration.

    What you should see

    HCN (Ka = 4.9×10⁻¹⁰) barely ionizes at all: about 1 molecule in 45 000. Its pH is near 5.2, just above that of water plus a trace of acid.

Worked example

Household vinegar is 5.0% acetic acid (mass per volume), which is about 0.83 M. Find its pH and percent ionization. Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. ICE. CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻. I: 0.83, 0, 0. C: −x, +x, +x. E: 0.83 − x, x, x.
  2. Write Ka. 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = x² / (0.83 − x).
  3. Try the shortcut. 0.83 − x ≈ 0.83, so x² = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.83 = 1.49 × 10⁻⁵ and x = 3.9 × 10⁻³ M. Check: 3.9 × 10⁻³ / 0.83 = 0.47%, under 5%. ✓
  4. Answer. pH = −log(3.9 × 10⁻³) = 2.41, and percent ionization = 0.47%. Real vinegar measures about pH 2.4 to 2.6.
  5. Compare. A strong acid at 0.83 M would have pH = −log 0.83 = 0.08. Acetic acid is weak: fewer than 1 molecule in 200 gives up its H⁺.

Common mistakes

  • Using [H₃O⁺] = c for a weak acid. That is only for strong acids.
  • Skipping the 5% check, especially for fairly strong weak acids (HF, HNO₂) at low concentration.
  • Reporting x as the pH instead of −log x.
  • Mixing up Ka and Kb: a weak base gives OH⁻, so x is [OH⁻] and you get pOH first.
  • Calling a weak acid "dilute" and a strong acid "concentrated". Strength (how much ionizes) is not the same as concentration (how much is there).

Why are some acids stronger than others?

Down a group (HF < HCl < HBr < HI) bond strength dominates: the longer, weaker H–X bond lets go of its proton more easily. Across a period (CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O < HF) electronegativity dominates. For oxyacids (HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄), more oxygen atoms (or more electronegative substituents) pull electron density away from the O–H bond and stabilize the conjugate base. In carboxylic acids, a chlorine on the carbon increases acidity.
