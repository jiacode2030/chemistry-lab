Electron configuration & PES

Electrons sit in shells and subshells, and a photoelectron spectrum is a photograph of that structure: one peak per subshell, as tall as the number of electrons in it.

The idea

Think of an apartment building. Each floor (shell) holds a limited number of people, and each floor is divided into rooms (subshells: s, p, d). Electrons fill the lowest-energy rooms first, one to a space before doubling up. That filling order gives the electron configuration, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ for oxygen.

In photoelectron spectroscopy you shine high-energy light on atoms and measure how much energy it takes to knock each electron out. Electrons close to the nucleus are held tightly, so they need a lot of energy; outer electrons need little. Each peak is a subshell. Its position tells you the binding energy; its height tells you how many electrons share that energy.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Neon fills its second shell completely. Count the peaks and compare their heights.

    What you should see

    Three peaks with height ratio 2 : 2 : 6, for 1s², 2s² and 2p⁶. The 1s peak is far to the left because those electrons are closest to the nucleus.

  2. Go from neon to sodium. What changes in the spectrum?

    What you should see

    A new, small peak (height 1) appears at very low energy: the lone 3s electron in a new shell. All the old peaks also move left (to higher energy) because the nucleus has one more proton pulling on every electron.

  3. Compare nitrogen and oxygen. How many unpaired electrons does each have?

    What you should see

    Nitrogen: 2p³, three unpaired electrons (one in each p box, Hund's rule). Oxygen: 2p⁴, so one box now holds a pair and only two electrons are unpaired.

Worked example

A PES of an element shows peaks with relative heights 2, 2, 6, 1 (from highest to lowest binding energy). Which element is it?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Each peak is a subshell. The heights are the numbers of electrons: 2, 2, 6 and 1.
  2. Assign them in order. From the highest energy (the most tightly held electrons), the order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.
  3. Total the electrons. 2 + 2 + 6 + 1 = 11 electrons. A neutral atom has equal protons, so Z = 11.
  4. Answer. The element is sodium. The 1-electron peak at the lowest energy is the valence electron in a new shell.

Common mistakes

  • Reading peak height as energy. Height is the number of electrons; position is the energy.
  • Forgetting that the axis runs backwards: the peak farthest left has the highest binding energy.
  • Pairing electrons in p orbitals before every box has one (Hund's rule says spread out first).
  • Thinking the 2s and 2p peaks are at the same energy. They are close, but 2p electrons are held a little less tightly.
  • Thinking binding energies stay put as Z grows. Every peak shifts to higher energy as the nucleus gains protons.

Filling the boxes

Three rules put electrons in boxes: Aufbau (fill the lowest-energy subshell first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d …), Pauli exclusion (a box holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins) and Hund's rule (in a set of equal-energy boxes, put one electron in each before pairing any).

The capacity of a subshell is 2(2ℓ + 1): s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10. A shell n holds 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18 and so on. In a PES, the peak for a full subshell has relative height 2 (s), 6 (p) or 10 (d).
