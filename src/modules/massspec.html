Mass spectra & average atomic mass

Atoms of the same element can have different masses. A mass spectrometer sorts them, and the number on the periodic table is the weighted average of what it finds.

The idea

Atoms of one element all have the same number of protons, but can have different numbers of neutrons. These versions are isotopes. Chlorine atoms come as ³⁵Cl (18 neutrons) and ³⁷Cl (20 neutrons). In a sample of chlorine, about three in four atoms are ³⁵Cl.

A mass spectrometer ionizes atoms and sends them through a magnetic field. Lighter ions bend more, so the instrument sorts the beam by mass, and a detector counts each kind. The result is a mass spectrum: one peak per isotope, with the height showing how common it is. Average the masses with each weighted by its abundance and you get the atomic mass on the periodic table. That is why it isn't a whole number.

Worked example

Copper has two isotopes: ⁶³Cu with mass 62.930 u (69.15%) and ⁶⁵Cu with mass 64.928 u (30.85%). Find the average atomic mass.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Turn percentages into fractions. 69.15% = 0.6915 and 30.85% = 0.3085. They add to 1.
  2. Weight each mass. 0.6915 × 62.930 = 43.52 and 0.3085 × 64.928 = 20.03.
  3. Add. 43.52 + 20.03 = 63.55 u.
  4. Check. The answer lies between 62.93 and 64.93 and is closer to 62.93, the more abundant isotope. ✓

Common mistakes

  • Averaging the isotope masses without weighting them (just adding and dividing by two).
  • Using percentages as if they were fractions: 69.15% must become 0.6915 before multiplying.
  • Thinking the atomic mass is the mass of the most common isotope. It is close, but not equal.
  • Believing a mass spectrum shows how many atoms there are. Peak heights show relative abundance, not total amount.
  • Mixing up mass number (protons + neutrons, a whole number) with isotopic mass (measured, close to a whole number).

Weighted averages

Average atomic mass = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass). The same idea gives your grade: tests that count more pull the average toward them. On the seesaw above, each isotope is a weight whose size is its abundance, and the balance point is the average.

In a spectrum with several peaks, read the mass of each peak from the horizontal axis and its abundance from the height, converting heights to fractions that sum to 1. If the heights are given as relative to the tallest peak, divide each by the sum of all heights first.
